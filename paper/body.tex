\section{Introduction}
Most of us work in noise without noticing it any more: the hum of an office air conditioner, a
compressor beside a production line, road noise inside a car, the chatter that surrounds someone wearing
a hearing aid. If a second microphone can be placed near the noise source, the noise arriving at the main
microphone can be predicted from it and subtracted. The predictor is an adaptive filter. It learns the
acoustic path between the two microphones while it runs and follows that path when it changes. For this
filter the least-mean-square (LMS) algorithm and its normalised form (NLMS) are still the default. They
cost only a few multiply--accumulate operations per tap and sample, and their behaviour has been studied
for decades~\cite{lu2021a}.

A small embedded device has to run this filter in real time, with a short and fixed delay, on very
little energy. A field-programmable gate array (FPGA) suits that job well. The datapath is built around
the algorithm, the latency is known to the clock cycle, and there is no processor or operating system to
schedule. In hardware, though, LMS and NLMS are not equally easy. LMS needs nothing beyond multipliers and
adders, but how fast it converges depends on how loud the input is. NLMS removes that dependence by
dividing the step size by the input power, which costs one division per sample. Dividers are slow and
take a lot of logic, so recent designs approximate the division instead: with an approximate
Newton--Raphson iteration~\cite{guidotti2020}, with canonical-signed-digit reciprocals~\cite{yao2024}, or
with a binary reciprocal built from a leading-one detector and a shifter~\cite{huang2026}. The crudest
option rounds the input power to a power of two, and the division turns into a plain shift. We call this
variant NLMS-Shift.

Two things kept bothering us as we read this literature. The first is tuning. Variants are rarely
tuned with the same care, so a reported gain may come from a weak baseline rather than from the
algorithm. We fell into this ourselves: an early version of this work showed NLMS-Shift 5.7~dB ahead of
NLMS, and the gap vanished once NLMS was given a sensible regularisation constant. The second is the link
between model and silicon. Hardware papers report area, clock rate and power. Those numbers show that a
design fits on the chip, not that the chip produces the same samples as the model behind the accuracy
results.

This paper describes ULLAP, a 12-bit, 32-tap adaptive noise canceller for the low-cost Terasic DE2-115
board (Intel Cyclone IV E) and its WM8731 audio codec, and uses it to address both concerns. Our main
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item One serial core that runs LMS, NLMS (with a restoring divider) and NLMS-Shift on a single shared
multiplier. The three algorithms therefore share the same datapath, word length and filter length. The
core sits in a complete audio system without a processor, closes timing at 50~MHz on Cyclone IV E, and is
released together with all scripts and data.
\item A fair comparison. Each algorithm's step size and regulariser are picked on held-out seeds by
the same rule, and every result carries a 20-seed 95\% confidence interval. The comparison covers
synthetic scenarios and real recorded noise and speech, and includes a reproducible word-length study.
\item A bit-exact verification chain from a Python integer model through the RTL to the board wrapper
(86 regression tests, codec, freeze and reset behaviour included). On the board, captures of real codec
samples show every output sample of all three algorithms equal to the model's.
\item Two explanations backed by measurement. The first concerns the shift. NLMS-Shift turns out to be
NLMS with a step that moves between 0.5 and 1 times the nominal one as the input level changes. NLMS run
with the average of that step reproduces NLMS-Shift within 0.3~dB on broadband noise, while on tonal input
the step jumps whenever the window power crosses a power of two. The second concerns the real recordings.
Once the speech is taken out, the same filters remove 18.5--31.9~dB of the same real noise. The lower
noise reduction with speech is therefore caused by the speech disturbing the adaptation, not by the
noise.
\end{enumerate}
The shift normaliser itself is not new, and we do not claim it; Section~\ref{sec:related} places it
among earlier division-free designs.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the problem and the related work. Section~\ref{sec:system} gives an
overview of the system, Section~\ref{sec:model} the algorithms and the fixed-point model, and
Section~\ref{sec:hw} the hardware. Sections~\ref{sec:verif} and~\ref{sec:setup} cover verification and
the experimental set-up, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, and Sections~\ref{sec:disc}
and~\ref{sec:concl} the discussion and conclusions.

\section{Literature review}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{The problem: noise in daily life}
Noise is no longer treated as a mere nuisance; it is an environmental health risk. In Europe more than
113 million people live with transport noise above 55~dB(A), and traffic noise alone is estimated to cost
about 1.3 million healthy life-years a year through its effect on the heart and blood
vessels~\cite{munzel2025}. Hahad et al.~\cite{hahad2025} review evidence that long exposure to noise is
also linked to mental-health disorders, and they discuss the biological routes that may explain it.
Hearing is the other side of the problem. The World Health Organization puts the number of people living
with some degree of hearing loss at about 1.5 billion, and expects it to reach 2.5 billion by
2050~\cite{chadha2021}. For these listeners background noise is the main reason speech stays hard to
follow, even with a hearing aid. Cutting noise at the microphone or near the ear is useful in many
settings: beside roads, where urban traffic noise sits mostly below 2~kHz~\cite{yang2024}; inside vehicle
cabins~\cite{jiang2023,zhu2024}; in ventilation ducts and air conditioners~\cite{zhu2025}; and in hearing
aids~\cite{yin2023,husstedt2023,tetteh2026}. Many of the devices involved are small, cheap and
battery-powered, so the processing has to run in real time at low cost and low energy.

\subsection{Applications of adaptive noise control}
Yang et al.~\cite{yang2024} recorded road traffic noise at five sites in Beijing and built a
feedforward LMS prototype that removes close to 30~dB(A) of single- and dual-frequency noise below 2~kHz.
Working in a car, Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang2023} made the FxLMS step depend on the reference norm and tuned
the two parameters of that rule by particle-swarm optimisation. On a dSPACE board their system reached up
to 12~dB below 100~Hz, and about 3~dB over 20--400~Hz in road tests, where plain FxLMS managed between
$-0.8$ and 0.3~dB. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2024} drove a car's own loudspeakers to control the first four
engine orders. They measured an average reduction of 6.2~dB at 52 ear-level microphones, and in a jury
test 93\% of the listeners felt more comfortable with the system on. In hearing aids, Yin and
Chen~\cite{yin2023} weighted the error of a variable-step adaptive feedback canceller; on a real device it
converged 35\% faster than a PEM-IMLMS baseline and 60\% faster than PEM-NLMS. Husstedt et
al.~\cite{husstedt2023} tested six commercial behind-the-ear devices and found that impulse noise reduction
lowers C-weighted peak levels by up to 20~dB, or about 9~dB on average. A recent systematic review of speech
enhancement for hearing aids~\cite{tetteh2026} finds that deep networks perform best in complex scenes,
while hybrids that keep an adaptive filter give a better balance of latency and computation on wearable
devices.

\subsection{Adaptive algorithms}
Lu et al.\ surveyed ten years of active noise control algorithms in two parts, one on linear
systems~\cite{lu2021a} and one on nonlinear systems~\cite{lu2021b}. In both, LMS-type updates remain the
base on which most newer methods are built. A popular idea is to run two filters at once, one fast and one
accurate, and combine them. F\'elix et al.~\cite{felix2021} proposed a modified convex combination of FXLMS
filters that converges faster and attenuates more than FXLMS and the earlier convex scheme. Zhu et
al.~\cite{zhu2025} joined an adaptive FxLMS filter and a CNN--GRU network through a convex weight to cancel
harmonic noise in a bent air-conditioner pipe. A different problem arises when the loudspeaker leaks back
into the reference microphone and the loop turns unstable. Li et al.~\cite{li2024} handled it with a
Youla-parameterised feedforward controller and an adaptive IIR update, tested on a duct. All of these
methods cancel better, but they add operations, networks or whole extra filters to every sample. That is
the opposite of what a small FPGA design needs.

\subsection{Hardware realisations}
On the hardware side, Thannoon and Hashim~\cite{thannoon2024} combined a fast retimed RLS filter and a
slow LMS filter on an FPGA to remove power-line interference from ECG records of the MIT-BIH database. They
report 24.2\% higher SNR and 50\% faster convergence than conventional structures. Jagadeeswari et
al.~\cite{jagadeeswari2026} applied cut-set retiming to delayed NLMS filters for audio on a Spartan-6 and
cut the critical path from 29.96 to 13.39~ns. For underwater sonar, Karakaya and Aytar~\cite{karakaya2026}
built semi-parallel 16-bit NLMS and variable-step LMS filters on an Artix-7, raising the SNR from 8.85~dB
to 29.36 and 28.57~dB with 2.51\% and 2.91\% of the LUTs. Park and Park~\cite{park2023} went a different
way: a small CNN classifies the noise and selects one of several fixed filters, which in simulation gave
2.3~dB more SNR than LMS and 53\% less dynamic power. Abdi et al.~\cite{abdi2024} built a narrowband
controller around a variable-step LMS notch filter on an FPGA to suppress microphonics in a
superconducting accelerator cavity. Other groups worked on the arithmetic itself. Nagabushanam et
al.~\cite{nagabushanam2025} built a multiplier-less LMS filter from distributed arithmetic with
offset-binary coding, and da Rosa et al.~\cite{rosa2022} replaced the multipliers of an LMS power-line
canceller with adders and shifters, saving up to 4.98 times in power and 30.46\% in area.

\subsection{Removing the division from NLMS}
Closest to our work are the papers that attack the NLMS division directly. Guidotti et
al.~\cite{guidotti2020} saved power in an NLMS interference canceller with an approximate Newton--Raphson
integer divider, and Coelho et al.~\cite{coelho2022} lowered the cost of adaptive filtering with
sum-of-powers-of-two arithmetic. Yao and Huang~\cite{yao2024} approximated the reciprocal of the input
power with canonical-signed-digit encoded divisors in a 16-tap NLMS FIR filter. Huang and
Yao~\cite{huang2026} then proposed a binary reciprocal approximation for an NLMS IIR filter. On the same
DE2-115 board we use, their design needs 5.07~nJ per sample against 8.33~nJ with a 15-bit SRT divider,
and it lowers the energy per sample by 39.1\%, 6.5\%, 14.4\% and 5.6\% relative to SRT, Newton--Raphson,
CSD and lookup-table methods~\cite{huang2026}. They also point out that the older idea of rounding the
divisor to a single power of two, which turns the division into a shift, has a large approximation
error. That plain shift is exactly NLMS-Shift. Section~\ref{sec:equiv} shows that its error acts only as
a level-dependent step factor between 0.5 and 1.

We found no work that compares LMS, a true divider and a shift normaliser on one core, with equal
tuning and statistical tests, and only a few that test a complete audio system with a codec on a board.
ULLAP adds that equal-effort three-way comparison, a bit-exact chain from the model to the hardware, and
an evaluation on real recordings.

\section{System overview}\label{sec:system}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the whole system. Noise from the source $v$ travels to the primary
microphone through an unknown acoustic path $h$ and mixes there with the wanted signal $s$. A reference
microphone placed near the source picks up $v$ itself. The two microphones feed the left and right
LINE IN channels of the WM8731 codec, which digitises them and passes them to the FPGA over an I$^2$S
link. In the FPGA, the I$^2$S receiver hands one pair $(x[n],d[n])$ per sample period to the ANC core.
The core returns the error $e[n]$, which is the cleaned signal, and the I$^2$S transmitter sends it back
to the codec DAC and LINE OUT. An I$^2$C engine programs the codec once after reset. Two slide switches
select bypass and freeze the adaptation, a push button resets the system, and four GPIO test points
bring out the sample strobe, the output strobe, the busy flag and the frame clock so that the timing can
be measured. Everything is plain Verilog; there is no processor and no vendor IP.

\section{Algorithms and fixed-point model}\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Signal model and cost function}
The reference microphone sees the noise alone. The primary microphone sees the speech, the noise after
the acoustic path, and a little sensor noise $n_s$:
\begin{align}
x[n] &= v[n], \label{eq:x}\\
d[n] &= s[n] + \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} h_j\, v[n-j] + n_s[n]. \label{eq:d}
\end{align}
Collecting the last $L$ reference samples in $\mathbf{x}[n]=[x[n],\dots,x[n-L+1]]^T$ and the weights in
$\mathbf{w}[n]=[w_0[n],\dots,w_{L-1}[n]]^T$, the filter estimates the noise part of $d[n]$, and what is
left over is the system output:
\begin{align}
y[n] &= \mathbf{w}^T[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n] = \sum_{i=0}^{L-1} w_i[n]\,x[n-i], \label{eq:y}\\
e[n] &= d[n]-y[n]. \label{eq:e}
\end{align}
Since $s$ is uncorrelated with $v$, minimising the mean-square error
\begin{align}
J(\mathbf{w}) &= E\{e^2[n]\} = E\{d^2[n]\} - 2\,\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}, \label{eq:J}\\
\mathbf{R} &= E\{\mathbf{x}[n]\,\mathbf{x}^T[n]\},\qquad \mathbf{p}=E\{d[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n]\}, \label{eq:Rp}
\end{align}
removes the noise and leaves the speech alone. The minimum is the Wiener solution
$\mathbf{w}_o=\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{p}$, which equals the acoustic path when $L\ge M$ and $n_s$ is
white.

\subsection{LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift}
LMS replaces the gradient $\nabla J=-2(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w})$ with its instantaneous
estimate $-2e[n]\mathbf{x}[n]$. All three algorithms use the resulting update
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}[n+1]=\mathbf{w}[n]+\mu[n]\,e[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n].
\label{eq:upd}
\end{equation}
and differ only in the step $\mu[n]$. With the window power $P[n]=\|\mathbf{x}[n]\|^2$, the steps of
LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift are
\begin{align}
\mu_{\text{LMS}}[n] &= \mu, & 0<\mu&<\frac{2}{L\,\sigma_x^2}, \label{eq:mu_lms}\\
\mu_{\text{NLMS}}[n] &= \frac{\tilde\mu}{P[n]+\epsilon}, & 0<\tilde\mu&<2, \label{eq:mu_nlms}\\
\mu_{\text{Shift}}[n] &= 2^{-(k[n]+K)}, && \label{eq:mu_shift}\\
k[n] &= \lfloor\log_2(P[n]+\epsilon)\rfloor+1, && \label{eq:k}
\end{align}
where $\sigma_x^2$ is the reference variance and $\epsilon>0$ is a small regulariser. The LMS bound
moves with the input level, which is why a fixed $\mu$ ends up either too slow for quiet inputs or
unstable for loud ones. The NLMS bound does not move. The hardware keeps the power up to date with the
exact recursion
\begin{equation}
P[n] = P[n-1] + x^2[n] - x^2[n-L],
\label{eq:prec}
\end{equation}
which needs two squarings per sample instead of $L$. In NLMS-Shift the exponent $k[n]$ is simply the
bit length of $P[n]+\epsilon$. A leading-one detector finds it, and multiplying by $\mu[n]$ becomes an
arithmetic right shift of $e[n]\,x[n-i]$ by $k[n]+K$ places.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bound}
For every $n$, the NLMS-Shift step \eqref{eq:mu_shift} satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{2^{-K}}{2\,(P[n]+\epsilon)} \;\le\; \mu_{\text{Shift}}[n] \;<\; \frac{2^{-K}}{P[n]+\epsilon},
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
In other words, the NLMS-Shift step never falls more than a factor of two below the NLMS step with
$\tilde\mu=2^{-K}$, and it respects the NLMS stability bound $0<\tilde\mu<2$ for $K\ge0$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Proof.} By \eqref{eq:k}, $2^{k[n]-1}\le P[n]+\epsilon<2^{k[n]}$. Taking reciprocals and
multiplying by $2^{-K}$ gives \eqref{eq:bound}, since $\mu_{\text{Shift}}[n]=2^{-K}\,2^{-k[n]}$.\hfill$\square$

\smallskip\noindent The exact relation is
\begin{equation}
\mu[n]=\frac{r[n]\,2^{-K}}{P[n]+\epsilon},\qquad
r[n]=\frac{P[n]+\epsilon}{2^{\lfloor\log_2(P[n]+\epsilon)\rfloor+1}}\in[0.5,1),
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
so NLMS-Shift is NLMS with the time-varying step $2^{-K}r[n]$ (Section~\ref{sec:equiv}). If the
input level is unknown over several octaves, the fractional part
$u=\log_2(P+\epsilon)-\lfloor\log_2(P+\epsilon)\rfloor$ is spread evenly over $[0,1)$, and the mean ratio
is
\begin{equation}
E\{r\}=\int_0^1 2^{u-1}\,du=\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\approx 0.72 .
\label{eq:er}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Fixed-point model}
Signals and weights are signed $Q1.F$ numbers with $W=12$ bits and $F=W-1$ fraction bits. Using the
rounding shift and the saturation
\begin{align}
\operatorname{rs}(a,m) &= \left\lfloor \frac{a+2^{m-1}}{2^m}\right\rfloor, \label{eq:rs}\\
\operatorname{sat}_B(a) &= \min\!\left(\max\!\left(a,-2^{B-1}\right),\,2^{B-1}-1\right), \label{eq:sat}
\end{align}
the integer datapath works on the integer samples $x_i=x[n-i]\cdot2^F$ and weights $w_i$ as
\begin{align}
a[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_{B_a}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{L-1} w_i\,x_i\Big), \label{eq:acc}\\
B_a &= 2W+\lceil\log_2 L\rceil, \label{eq:ba}\\
y[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_W\big(\operatorname{rs}(a[n],F)\big), \label{eq:yq}\\
e[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_W\big(d[n]-y[n]\big), \label{eq:ye}\\
w_i &\leftarrow \operatorname{sat}_W\big(w_i+\Delta w_i\big), \label{eq:wupd}
\end{align}
with the weight increments
\begin{align}
\Delta w_i^{\text{LMS}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big((M_\mu\,e[n])\,x_i,\;F+10\big), \label{eq:dw_lms}\\
\Delta w_i^{\text{NLMS}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big(g[n]\,x_i,\;F\big), \label{eq:dw_nlms}\\
g[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_{W+4}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2N[n]+Q[n]}{2Q[n]}\right\rfloor\right), \label{eq:g}\\
\Delta w_i^{\text{Shift}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big(e[n]\,x_i,\;b(Q[n])-F+K\big), \label{eq:dw_shift}
\end{align}
for LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift. Here $\mu=M_\mu\,2^{-10}$, $N[n]=M_\mu\,e[n]\,2^{2F-10}$,
$Q[n]=P[n]+2^{2F-E}$ for $\epsilon=2^{-E}$, and $b(\cdot)$ is the bit length of a positive integer. Every
point where a value is narrowed saturates and sets a sticky flag, so an overflow can never go unnoticed.
The integer model (\texttt{py/fixedpoint.py}, \texttt{py/rtl\_model.py}) uses exactly these widths and
produces the golden vectors.

\section{Hardware architecture}\label{sec:hw}
\subsection{Core}\label{sec:core}
Fig.~\ref{fig:core} shows the datapath of the core (\texttt{rtl/anc\_core.v}, vendor-neutral Verilog).
The core takes one sample pair per \texttt{in\_valid} strobe and does all its arithmetic on one
multiplier with a three-stage pipeline (operand registers, product register, consume stage). A
finite-state machine walks through each sample. It spends two cycles squaring the new and the dropped
reference sample for \eqref{eq:prec} (NLMS and NLMS-Shift only), $L$ cycles on the filter sum
\eqref{eq:acc} and one cycle on $y$ and $e$ \eqref{eq:ye}. The step computation follows, and then $L$
cycles of weight updates \eqref{eq:wupd}. NLMS adds a sequential restoring divider for the gain $g[n]$;
NLMS-Shift has a leading-one detector and a shift-amount register in its place. A \texttt{freeze} input
holds the weights while filtering goes on.

This schedule fixes both the input-to-output latency and the number of busy cycles per sample:
\begin{align}
N_{\text{lat}} &= \begin{cases} L+3 & \text{LMS},\\ L+5 & \text{NLMS, NLMS-Shift},\end{cases} \label{eq:lat}\\
N_{\text{busy}} &= \begin{cases} 2L+9 & \text{LMS},\\ 2L+13+N_{\text{div}} & \text{NLMS},\\ 2L+8 & \text{NLMS-Shift},\end{cases} \label{eq:busy}
\end{align}
The restoring divider runs one iteration per dividend bit,
$N_{\text{div}}=W+B_\mu+2F+4+\lceil\log_2 L\rceil$, where $B_\mu$ is the signed width of $M_\mu$, so the
NLMS cycle count grows with the step-size constant. For $L=32$ and the tuned settings the formulas give
latencies of 35, 37 and 37 cycles and busy times of 73, 130 ($N_{\text{div}}=53$) and 72 cycles, the same
values we measured on the board (Section~\ref{sec:board}).

\subsection{Board wrapper}
Around the core, the DE2-115 wrapper holds an I$^2$C engine that sets up the WM8731 at reset (11
registers, each write acknowledge-checked), an I$^2$S receiver and transmitter, two-flop synchronisers on
the reset and the switches, a frame-latched bypass multiplexer, an overrun flag, and four registered test
points on the GPIO header. The I$^2$S blocks run on the 50~MHz system clock and sample the codec's bit and
word clocks as data. The codec is the clock master, driven from a 12.5~MHz clock with its sample-rate
register at 1536~fs, so
\begin{align}
f_s &= \frac{f_{\text{MCLK}}}{1536}=\frac{12.5~\text{MHz}}{1536}=8.138~\text{kHz}, \label{eq:fs}\\
N_s &= \frac{f_{\text{clk}}}{f_s}=\frac{50~\text{MHz}}{8.138~\text{kHz}}=6144, \label{eq:ns}
\end{align}
which is the rate used in the simulations, with $N_s$ clock cycles per sample. The core is busy for the
fraction
\begin{equation}
U=\frac{N_{\text{busy}}}{N_s}
\label{eq:util}
\end{equation}
of each sample period. By \eqref{eq:busy} that is 1.19\%, 2.12\% and 1.17\% for LMS, NLMS and
NLMS-Shift. Again, no processor or vendor IP is involved.

\section{Experimental set-up}\label{sec:setup}
The synthetic scenarios follow the reference model $x=v$, $d=s+h*v+n_s$ at 8~kHz with 16,000
samples. There are four: stationary coloured noise, amplitude steps ($\times3$ then $\times0.25$), an
abrupt path switch at the midpoint, and a recorded chord as the noise source. We chose step sizes and
regularisers on seeds 101--103, maximising the mean final noise reduction over the stationary,
amplitude-step and path-switch scenarios with no overflow allowed. This gave LMS $\mu=0.25$; NLMS
$\mu=0.25$, $\epsilon=1$; and NLMS-Shift $K=3$, $\epsilon=0.25$. All reported results use seeds 1--20.
The final noise reduction is taken over the last quarter $\mathcal{N}$ of the samples,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NR}=10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\big(d[n]-s[n]\big)^2}{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\big(e[n]-s[n]\big)^2}\ \text{dB},
\label{eq:nr}
\end{equation}
and each table entry gives the mean over the $S=20$ seeds with its 95\% confidence interval,
\begin{equation}
\overline{\mathrm{NR}}\pm t_{0.975,\,S-1}\,\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{NR}}}{\sqrt{S}},\qquad t_{0.975,19}=2.093 .
\label{eq:ci}
\end{equation}
To compare two algorithms A and B we use the paired per-seed differences
$\Delta_k=\mathrm{NR}_{\mathrm{A},k}-\mathrm{NR}_{\mathrm{B},k}$. We call them equivalent when two
one-sided tests (TOST) both reject, at $p<0.05$, that the mean difference lies outside a margin
$\pm\delta$ with $\delta=0.5$~dB:
\begin{equation}
H_{01}:\ \bar\Delta\le-\delta,\qquad H_{02}:\ \bar\Delta\ge\delta .
\label{eq:tost}
\end{equation}
\emph{Use of generative AI.} The authors used Claude (Anthropic) as an assistant throughout this work:
to write and debug analysis scripts and testbench code, to search for and summarise literature, and to
draft and edit the text of this manuscript. \TBD{Authors: add any other AI tools you used (for example
for local runs or literature searches) and adjust the list above if needed.} The authors checked every
reported number against the committed result files and board captures, checked each reference against
its source, and take full responsibility for the content of this article.

For the real recordings we took four noise files (air conditioner, vacuum cleaner, copy machine,
babble) and ten utterances from the public MS-SNSD
corpus\footnote{\url{https://github.com/microsoft/MS-SNSD}} and resampled them to 8~kHz. Each seed draws a
noise segment, an utterance and a random 16-tap acoustic path, at 0~dB input SNR, with and without
speech leaking into the reference at $-20$~dB. Noise reduction, the short-time objective intelligibility
(STOI) measure and the narrow-band perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ, ITU-T Recommendation
P.862) are computed after a 2~s convergence window.

\subsection{Equivalent step size of NLMS-Shift}\label{sec:equiv}
By \eqref{eq:ratio}, NLMS-Shift and NLMS differ only in the factor $r[n]$. Averaged over input levels
spread evenly on a logarithmic scale, $E[r]=\int_0^1 2^{u-1}\,du=1/(2\ln 2)\approx0.72$. At any one level,
though, $r$ depends on where $P+\epsilon$ sits inside its octave: it grows with the level and halves when
$P+\epsilon$ crosses a power of two. With $L=32$ and $\epsilon=2^{-2}$, the crossing $P+\epsilon=1/2$
falls at a reference RMS of $-21.1$~dBFS, 3~dB above the level used in the simulations. To see the
effect, we swept the input level over 12~dB in 0.75~dB steps in the integer model
(\texttt{py/exp\_equiv\_step.py}), on the stationary scenario (seeds 1--5) and on a two-tone reference
(Fig.~\ref{fig:equiv}). On broadband noise the window power varies enough to smooth out the sawtooth, and
the mean ratio $\bar r$ moves gradually between 0.56 and 0.78. On the tonal reference $P$ is almost
constant, and $\bar r$ drops from 0.87 to 0.62 within 0.75~dB of level. In both cases NLMS with the
constant step $\bar r\,2^{-K}$ reproduces NLMS-Shift. The final noise reduction agrees within 0.27~dB at
all 17 broadband levels, and within 0.55~dB at every tonal level except the one that straddles the
crossing (1.7~dB); the convergence time agrees within 8\%. NLMS with the nominal step $2^{-K}$, by
contrast, is off by up to 3.5~dB and 48\%.

The shift, then, changes nothing in the adaptation except the step size. It turns the fixed NLMS step
into a step between $0.5$ and $1$ times the nominal one, set by the input level. On broadband input this
is a smooth change of gain, and choosing $K$ (and $\epsilon$) for the expected level absorbs it. On tonal
input the step changes by up to a factor of about 0.7 when the level crosses a power-of-two boundary of
$P+\epsilon$, something a fixed-step NLMS never does.

LMS is about 31\% smaller than either normalised variant. NLMS-Shift and NLMS need about the same logic
(2,610 and 2,626 logic elements): the area saved on the divider goes to the leading-one detector, the

constraint with at least +2.953~ns setup slack and +0.401~ns hold slack, and without critical warnings. At
8.138~kHz even the busiest variant uses only 2\% of the 6,144-cycle sample period, so one core could
serve many channels or much longer filters.

The power figures are Quartus vectorless estimates with the default 12.5\% toggle rate, which Quartus
itself rates as low confidence. Core static power (about 98.7~mW) dominates the 169 to 173~mW total. The
dynamic estimates rank the designs LMS $<$ NLMS-Shift $<$ NLMS, in the same order as the toggle counts.

second, while ULLAP shares one multiplier and runs at 8.138~kHz, so area and energy have to be compared
per tap and per sample, not as raw totals.



\subsection{On-board results}\label{sec:board}
We recorded the board evidence with Signal Tap on the FPGA itself, so no external recorder or
oscilloscope was needed. One logic-analyser instance stores, once per sample, the two codec samples
entering the core ($x$ and $d$, register outputs \texttt{x\_reg} and \texttt{d\_reg}) and the core output
$e$. It uses a transitional storage qualifier on \texttt{out\_valid}, triggers when the reset button is
released, and holds 32,768 samples (4.0~s). A second instance samples the four GPIO test points at
50~MHz. The stimuli were the noise-only versions of the four scenarios, played from a laptop into LINE IN
after a 1~kHz calibration tone. We pressed reset during the tone, so every capture starts from zero
weights on real codec data at about $-25$~dBFS, and no sample in any capture reaches full scale. The
analysis script and the raw exports are in the repository (\texttt{py/analyze\_board\_capture.py},
\texttt{board\_evidence/}).

\emph{Bit-exactness.} We took each of the 12 captures (three algorithms, four scenarios), started the
integer model of Section~\ref{sec:model} from zero state at the first sample after the reset, and fed it
the captured $x$ and $d$. It reproduced every captured $e[n]$: 351,723 samples in total, 28,672 to
32,768 (3.5 to 4.0~s) per capture, without a single mismatch. The fixed-point arithmetic on the chip is
therefore exactly the arithmetic we evaluated in simulation, even with analogue input carrying the
codec's noise and offsets.

\emph{Timing.} The GPIO capture shows an \texttt{in\_valid} period of 6,144 clock cycles
(8,138.02~Hz), an \texttt{in\_valid} to \texttt{out\_valid} spacing of 38, 36 and 38 cycles for
NLMS-Shift, LMS and NLMS (the core latency plus one accept cycle, 0.76, 0.72 and 0.76~$\mu$s), and busy
times of 72, 73 and 130 cycles. All of these equal the RTL simulation with the same parameters. The
builds with Signal Tap still meet the 50~MHz constraint (setup slack $+2.65$, $+5.68$ and $+3.78$~ns);
Table~\ref{tab:hw} refers to the builds without the logic analyser.

Fig.~\ref{fig:board} shows the three algorithms on the same amplitude-step stimulus, aligned to the
start of the scenario. (KEY0 was pressed by hand during the calibration tone, so the reset lands at a
slightly different point of the stimulus in each capture.) All three settle to about 15~dB in the first
1.5~s. At 2.1~s the level of $d$ rises by 7.4~dB, and they follow the step with only a short burst in $e$.
The residual power after the step ($-47$ to $-52$~dBFS) is lower than before it ($-44$~dBFS), so the
noise reduction climbs to 25 to 30~dB. Over the last quarter of the window the reduction is about 40~dB
below 1.5~kHz and 11 to 15~dB near the band edge (3.5 to 3.9~kHz).

\emph{Noise reduction on the simulation signal.} In a second session we played the 2~s, seed-1
noise-only signal of each scenario, i.e.\ exactly the 16,000-sample signal used in the simulations,
once after the calibration tone, so that the whole scenario fits inside the capture. We located its
start by correlating the captured $x$ with the stimulus (correlation 0.95 to 0.99) and took
$\mathrm{NR}_\mathrm{final}$ over the same last 25~\% as in simulation. All 12 captures (28,672 samples
after the reset each) are again bit-exact. Table~\ref{tab:board} sets the board against the integer model
on the same signal. The board comes out 2.9 to 10.6~dB below the simulation, by similar amounts for all
three algorithms. The cause is the input level, not the hardware. The codec delivered $x$ and $d$ at about
$-27$~dBFS, 3~dB below the simulation level, and at the tuned step sizes a lower input power slows
adaptation within the 2~s. Run at the level the board actually received, the model matches the board
within 0.2~dB on stationary and path-switch noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:boardlevel}). Amplitude steps and the
recorded noise end with a quiet final quarter ($d$ at about $-41$ and $-57$~dBFS), and there the board
stays 1.7 to 8.1~dB below the level-matched model. At those levels two analogue effects are no longer
small against $d$: the noise of the playback and codec path ($-70$~dBFS, measured in the silence after
the stimulus) and an ADC offset that decays during the first second after the reset. For amplitude steps,
even the best 32-tap least-squares filter fitted to the board samples of that window reaches only 24~dB,
against 28~dB on the ideal signal. On the board, the three algorithms stay within 0.7~dB of one another
in every scenario.

($\mathrm{SW}[0]=1$) its upper 12 bits equal $d$, in all 1,792 samples of each capture. We could not
record the LINE OUT signal with the equipment we had, so this check takes the chain as far as the DAC
interface. After 10~minutes of continuous operation on looped noise the GPIO timing had not changed
(6,144 cycles per sample, 38 cycles from \texttt{in\_valid} to \texttt{out\_valid}, 72 busy cycles), and
no saturation, NACK or overrun flag was lit. The build with all three logic-analyser instances still met
timing (slack $+2.93$~ns).

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:disc}
Our comparison supports a narrow conclusion. The divider-free normalisation keeps the
level-independence of NLMS at roughly the cycle count of LMS. It is therefore the better choice when the
noise level changes and throughput or energy per sample matters, while LMS remains the smallest design
when the level is steady. It does not show that NLMS-Shift cancels better than NLMS, and
Section~\ref{sec:equiv} explains why. The shift only changes the step size, by a level-dependent factor
between 0.5 and 1, so choosing $K$ and $\epsilon$ for the expected input level matters more than the
shift itself. Tonal noise near a power-of-two boundary of $P+\epsilon$ also sees a step jump that NLMS
does not have. On real recordings, the speech at the primary input limits all three algorithms far more
than the choice of algorithm does (6.4--17.9~dB, against at most 0.6~dB between algorithms).

The study has limits. The acoustic paths are simulated, also for the real recordings. Acoustic feedback
(closed-loop howling) is not covered, and reference-based ANC is not the right structure for it anyway.
Power is a switching-activity proxy until a gate-level analysis is done. On the board, the output is
shown equal to the model up to the DAC word, but the analogue LINE OUT signal was not recorded. Finally,
the absolute noise reduction depends on the input level. With the stimulus 3~dB below the simulation
level, the board reached 14 to 17~dB instead of 19 to 28~dB on the synthetic scenarios, and on stationary
and path-switch noise the model reproduces this at that level. In a deployment, the codec input gain
should therefore be set so that the reference uses most of the 12-bit range, or the step size should be
retuned for the expected level.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
ULLAP runs LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift on one serial 12-bit, 32-tap core on a low-cost Cyclone IV board,
and compares them with equal tuning effort. NLMS-Shift matches NLMS within the 95\% confidence interval
on stationary and amplitude-step noise while needing 72 instead of 130 busy cycles per sample. All three
remove 11.7 to 14.9~dB of real recorded noise, and 18.5 to 31.9~dB once the speech is taken out of the
primary input, which shows that the speech, not the noise, sets the limit. NLMS-Shift is equivalent to
NLMS with a step between 0.5 and 1 times the nominal one that depends on the input level. On the board,
25 Signal Tap captures of real codec data (724,459 samples) are bit-exact with the integer model, and so
are the freeze behaviour and the DAC word. On stationary and path-switch noise, the measured noise
reduction equals the model's, at the input level the board received, within 0.2~dB.
