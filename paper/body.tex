\section{Introduction}
Unwanted sound is part of almost every working day: the hum of an air conditioner in an office, the
compressor next to a production line, the road noise in a car cabin, the babble around a person who wears
a hearing aid. When a second sensor can pick up the noise close to its source, the noise that reaches the
main microphone can be predicted from that reference and subtracted. An adaptive filter learns the
acoustic path between the two sensors while it runs, so it keeps working when the path or the noise
changes. The least-mean-square (LMS) algorithm and its normalised form (NLMS) are still the usual choice
for this filter, because each sample costs only a few multiply--accumulate operations per tap and the
behaviour is well understood~\cite{lu2021a}.

For small embedded devices the filter has to run in real time with a fixed, short delay and very little
energy, and here a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) is attractive: the datapath is built for the
algorithm, the latency is fixed to the clock cycle, and no processor or operating system is needed. The
two algorithms are not equally convenient in hardware, though. LMS needs only multiplications and
additions, but its convergence speed depends on the input level. NLMS divides the step size by the input
power and so removes this dependence, at the price of one division per sample. A divider is slow and
large in logic, and much recent work therefore replaces it by an approximation: an approximate
Newton--Raphson iteration~\cite{guidotti2020}, canonical-signed-digit reciprocals~\cite{yao2024}, or a
binary reciprocal built from a leading-one detector and a shifter~\cite{huang2026}. The simplest member
of this family rounds the input power to a power of two, so that the division becomes a plain shift; we
call this variant NLMS-Shift.

Reading this literature, we found two things missing. First, the variants are seldom compared with the
same tuning effort. A reported gain can then come from a badly tuned baseline rather than from the
algorithm. This happened in an earlier version of our own work: a 5.7~dB advantage of NLMS-Shift over NLMS
disappeared once NLMS was given a fair regularisation constant. Second, hardware papers report area,
clock rate and power, which show that a design fits on the chip, but they rarely show that the chip
computes the same samples as the model that produced the accuracy figures.

This paper presents ULLAP, a 12-bit, 32-tap adaptive noise canceller for the low-cost Terasic DE2-115
board (Intel Cyclone IV E) with its WM8731 audio codec, and uses it to answer both points. The main
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A single serial core that implements LMS, NLMS (restoring divider) and NLMS-Shift with one
shared multiplier, so the three are compared on identical datapath, word length and filter length.
\item A fair comparison: every algorithm's step size and regulariser are chosen on held-out seeds
with the same criterion, and results are reported with 20-seed 95\% confidence intervals,
on synthetic scenarios and on real recorded noise and speech.
\item A bit-exact verification chain from a Python integer model to the RTL and the board wrapper
(86 regression tests including codec, freeze and reset behaviour), and on-board captures of real codec
samples in which every output sample of all three algorithms equals the model's.
\item An exact account of what the shift changes: NLMS-Shift is NLMS with a level-dependent step
between 0.5 and 1 times the nominal one, and NLMS run with the mean of that step reproduces it within
0.3~dB on broadband noise; on tonal input the step jumps at power-of-two boundaries of the window power.
\item A measured account of the real-recording result: with the speech removed, the same filters remove
18.5--31.9~dB of the same real noise, so the lower noise reduction with speech comes from the speech
disturbing the adaptation, not from the noise.
\item A reproducible word-length study and a complete processor-free audio system that closes
timing at 50~MHz on Cyclone IV E, released with all scripts and data.
\end{enumerate}
We do not claim the shift normaliser itself as new; Section~\ref{sec:related} places it among earlier
division-free designs.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the problem and the
related work. Section~\ref{sec:system} gives the system overview, Section~\ref{sec:model} the algorithms
and the fixed-point model, and Section~\ref{sec:hw} the hardware architecture. Sections~\ref{sec:verif}
and~\ref{sec:setup} describe the verification and the experiments, Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
results, and Sections~\ref{sec:disc} and~\ref{sec:concl} discuss them and conclude.

\section{Literature review}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{The problem: noise in daily life}
Noise is now treated as an environmental health risk and not only as a nuisance. More than 113 million
people in Europe live with transport noise above 55~dB(A), and traffic noise alone is estimated to cost
about 1.3 million healthy life-years every year through its effect on the heart and blood
vessels~\cite{munzel2025}. Hahad et al.~\cite{hahad2025} review the evidence that long exposure to noise
is also linked to mental-health disorders and discuss the biological routes proposed for this link. The
World Health Organization estimates that about 1.5 billion people live with some degree of hearing loss,
a number that may reach 2.5 billion by 2050~\cite{chadha2021}. For these listeners, background noise is
the main reason why speech stays hard to follow even with a hearing aid. Reducing noise close to the ear
or at the microphone is therefore useful in many places: at the roadside, where measured urban traffic
noise lies mostly below 2~kHz~\cite{yang2024}; in vehicle cabins~\cite{jiang2023,zhu2024}; in ventilation
ducts and air conditioners~\cite{zhu2025}; and in hearing aids~\cite{yin2023,husstedt2023,tetteh2026}.
Many of these devices are small, battery-powered and cheap, so the processing must be both real-time and
low in cost and energy.

\subsection{Applications of adaptive noise control}
Yang et al.~\cite{yang2024} measured road traffic noise at five sites in Beijing and built a feedforward
LMS prototype that removes close to 30~dB(A) of single- and dual-frequency noise below 2~kHz. In a car,
Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang2023} let the FxLMS step depend on the reference norm, tuned the two parameters of
that rule with particle-swarm optimisation, and tested the system on a dSPACE board: it reached up to
12~dB below 100~Hz and about 3~dB over 20--400~Hz in road tests, where plain FxLMS gave between $-0.8$ and
0.3~dB. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2024} used the built-in loudspeakers of a car to control the first four engine
orders, measured an average reduction of 6.2~dB at 52 ear-level microphones, and found that 93\% of the
listeners in a jury test felt more comfortable with the system switched on. For hearing aids, Yin and
Chen~\cite{yin2023} weighted the error of a variable-step adaptive feedback canceller and converged 35\%
faster than a PEM-IMLMS baseline and 60\% faster than PEM-NLMS on a real device. Husstedt et
al.~\cite{husstedt2023} measured six commercial behind-the-ear devices and found that impulse noise
reduction lowers C-weighted peak levels by up to 20~dB, about 9~dB on average. A recent systematic review
of speech enhancement for hearing aids~\cite{tetteh2026} concludes that deep networks do best in complex
scenes, while hybrid systems that keep an adaptive filter offer a better trade-off between latency and
computation for wearable devices.

\subsection{Adaptive algorithms}
Lu et al.\ surveyed a decade of active noise control algorithms in two parts, one for linear
systems~\cite{lu2021a} and one for nonlinear systems~\cite{lu2021b}; in both, LMS-type updates remain the
base on which most newer methods are built. Several groups combine two filters to get fast convergence and
low steady-state error at the same time. F\'elix et al.~\cite{felix2021} proposed a modified convex
combination of FXLMS filters and report faster convergence and more attenuation than FXLMS and the
earlier convex scheme. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2025} combined an adaptive FxLMS filter with a CNN--GRU
network through a convex weight to cancel harmonic noise in a bent air-conditioner pipe. When the
loudspeaker feeds back into the reference microphone, the loop can become unstable; Li et
al.~\cite{li2024} addressed this with a Youla-parameterised feedforward controller and an adaptive IIR
update, and verified it on a duct. These methods improve the cancellation, but they add operations,
networks or extra filters to every sample, which is the opposite of what a small FPGA design needs.

\subsection{Hardware realisations}
Thannoon and Hashim~\cite{thannoon2024} implemented a convex combination of a fast retimed RLS filter and
a slow LMS filter on an FPGA to remove power-line interference from ECG records of the MIT-BIH database,
and report 24.2\% higher SNR and 50\% faster convergence than conventional structures. Jagadeeswari et
al.~\cite{jagadeeswari2026} applied cut-set retiming to delayed NLMS filters for audio on a Spartan-6 and
shortened the critical path from 29.96 to 13.39~ns. Karakaya and Aytar~\cite{karakaya2026} built
semi-parallel 16-bit NLMS and variable-step LMS filters on an Artix-7 for underwater sonar and raised the
SNR from 8.85~dB to 29.36 and 28.57~dB with 2.51\% and 2.91\% of the LUTs. Park and Park~\cite{park2023}
replaced the adaptive filter by fixed filters selected by a small CNN noise classifier and report
2.3~dB more SNR than LMS and 53\% less dynamic power in simulation. Abdi et al.~\cite{abdi2024} built a
narrowband controller with a variable-step LMS notch filter on an FPGA to suppress microphonics in a
superconducting accelerator cavity. At the arithmetic level, Nagabushanam et al.~\cite{nagabushanam2025}
used distributed arithmetic with offset-binary coding to build a multiplier-less LMS filter, and da Rosa
et al.~\cite{rosa2022} replaced the multipliers of an LMS power-line canceller by adders and shifters,
saving up to 4.98 times in power and 30.46\% in area.

\subsection{Removing the division from NLMS}
The works closest to ours deal with the NLMS division. Guidotti et al.~\cite{guidotti2020} used an
approximate Newton--Raphson integer divider in an NLMS interference canceller to save power, and Coelho et
al.~\cite{coelho2022} reduced the cost of adaptive filtering with sum-of-powers-of-two arithmetic. Yao and
Huang~\cite{yao2024} approximated the reciprocal of the input power with canonical-signed-digit encoded
divisors in a 16-tap NLMS FIR filter, and Huang and Yao~\cite{huang2026} later proposed a binary reciprocal
approximation for an NLMS IIR filter. On the same DE2-115 board used here, their design needs 5.07~nJ per
sample, against 8.33~nJ with a 15-bit SRT divider, and lowers the energy per sample by 39.1\%, 6.5\%,
14.4\% and 5.6\% relative to SRT, Newton--Raphson, CSD and lookup-table methods~\cite{huang2026}. They also
note that the earlier idea of quantising the divisor to a single power of two, which makes the division
a shift, gives a large approximation error. That plain shift is exactly NLMS-Shift; Section~\ref{sec:equiv}
shows that its error acts only as a level-dependent step factor between 0.5 and 1.

None of the works above compares LMS, a true divider and a shift normaliser on one core with equal tuning
and statistical tests, and few test a complete audio system with a codec on a board. ULLAP contributes
this equal-effort three-way comparison, the bit-exact chain from the model to the hardware, and an
evaluation on real recordings.

\section{System overview}\label{sec:system}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the complete system. The noise source $v$ reaches the primary microphone
through an unknown acoustic path $h$ and mixes there with the wanted signal $s$; a reference microphone
close to the source picks up $v$ itself. The two microphone signals enter the left and right LINE IN
channels of the WM8731 codec, which digitises them and sends them to the FPGA over an I$^2$S link. Inside
the FPGA, the I$^2$S receiver delivers one pair $(x[n],d[n])$ per sample period to the ANC core, the core
returns the error $e[n]$, which is the cleaned signal, and the I$^2$S transmitter sends it back to the codec
DAC and LINE OUT. An I$^2$C engine programs the codec once after reset. Two slide switches select bypass and
freeze the adaptation, a push button resets the system, and four test points on the GPIO header expose
the sample strobe, the output strobe, the busy flag and the frame clock for timing measurements. The
whole design is written in plain Verilog and uses neither a processor nor vendor IP.

\section{Algorithms and fixed-point model}\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Signal model and cost function}
The reference sensor observes the noise, and the primary sensor observes the speech plus the noise
filtered by the acoustic path and a small sensor noise $n_s$:
\begin{align}
x[n] &= v[n], \label{eq:x}\\
d[n] &= s[n] + \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} h_j\, v[n-j] + n_s[n]. \label{eq:d}
\end{align}
With the reference vector $\mathbf{x}[n]=[x[n],\dots,x[n-L+1]]^T$ and the weights
$\mathbf{w}[n]=[w_0[n],\dots,w_{L-1}[n]]^T$, the filter estimates the noise part of $d[n]$, and the error
is the system output:
\begin{align}
y[n] &= \mathbf{w}^T[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n] = \sum_{i=0}^{L-1} w_i[n]\,x[n-i], \label{eq:y}\\
e[n] &= d[n]-y[n]. \label{eq:e}
\end{align}
Because $s$ is uncorrelated with $v$, minimising the mean-square error
\begin{align}
J(\mathbf{w}) &= E\{e^2[n]\} = E\{d^2[n]\} - 2\,\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}, \label{eq:J}\\
\mathbf{R} &= E\{\mathbf{x}[n]\,\mathbf{x}^T[n]\},\qquad \mathbf{p}=E\{d[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n]\}, \label{eq:Rp}
\end{align}
removes the noise without touching the speech. Its minimum is the Wiener solution
$\mathbf{w}_o=\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{p}$, which for $L\ge M$ and white $n_s$ equals the acoustic path.

\subsection{LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift}
LMS replaces the gradient $\nabla J=-2(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w})$ by its instantaneous estimate
$-2e[n]\mathbf{x}[n]$, and all three algorithms share the resulting update
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}[n+1]=\mathbf{w}[n]+\mu[n]\,e[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n].
\label{eq:upd}
\end{equation}
They differ only in the step $\mu[n]$. Writing the window power as $P[n]=\|\mathbf{x}[n]\|^2$, the steps
of LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift are
\begin{align}
\mu_{\text{LMS}}[n] &= \mu, & 0<\mu&<\frac{2}{L\,\sigma_x^2}, \label{eq:mu_lms}\\
\mu_{\text{NLMS}}[n] &= \frac{\tilde\mu}{P[n]+\epsilon}, & 0<\tilde\mu&<2, \label{eq:mu_nlms}\\
\mu_{\text{Shift}}[n] &= 2^{-(k[n]+K)}, && \label{eq:mu_shift}\\
k[n] &= \lfloor\log_2(P[n]+\epsilon)\rfloor+1, && \label{eq:k}
\end{align}
where $\sigma_x^2$ is the reference variance and $\epsilon>0$ a small regulariser. The LMS bound depends
on the input level, which is why a fixed $\mu$ is either slow at low levels or unstable at high levels;
the NLMS bound does not. For the power, the hardware uses the exact recursion
\begin{equation}
P[n] = P[n-1] + x^2[n] - x^2[n-L],
\label{eq:prec}
\end{equation}
which costs two squarings per sample instead of $L$. In NLMS-Shift the exponent $k[n]$ is the bit length
of $P[n]+\epsilon$, so it follows from a leading-one detector, and the multiplication by $\mu[n]$ becomes
an arithmetic right shift of $e[n]\,x[n-i]$ by $k[n]+K$ places. Since
$2^{k[n]-1}\le P[n]+\epsilon<2^{k[n]}$, the step is bounded by
\begin{equation}
\frac{2^{-K}}{2\,(P[n]+\epsilon)} \;\le\; \mu_{\text{Shift}}[n] \;<\; \frac{2^{-K}}{P[n]+\epsilon},
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
so NLMS-Shift always lies within a factor of two below NLMS with $\tilde\mu=2^{-K}$ and keeps the NLMS
stability bound. More exactly,
\begin{equation}
\mu[n]=\frac{r[n]\,2^{-K}}{P[n]+\epsilon},\qquad
r[n]=\frac{P[n]+\epsilon}{2^{\lfloor\log_2(P[n]+\epsilon)\rfloor+1}}\in[0.5,1),
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
so NLMS-Shift is NLMS with the time-varying step $2^{-K}r[n]$ (Section~\ref{sec:equiv}). If
$u=\log_2(P+\epsilon)-\lfloor\log_2(P+\epsilon)\rfloor$ is spread uniformly over $[0,1)$, which holds when
the input level is unknown over several octaves, the mean ratio is
\begin{equation}
E\{r\}=\int_0^1 2^{u-1}\,du=\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\approx 0.72 .
\label{eq:er}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Fixed-point model}
All signals and weights are signed $Q1.F$ numbers with $W=12$ bits and $F=W-1$ fraction bits. With the
rounding shift and the saturation
\begin{align}
\operatorname{rs}(a,m) &= \left\lfloor \frac{a+2^{m-1}}{2^m}\right\rfloor, \label{eq:rs}\\
\operatorname{sat}_B(a) &= \min\!\left(\max\!\left(a,-2^{B-1}\right),\,2^{B-1}-1\right), \label{eq:sat}
\end{align}
the integer datapath computes, for integer samples $x_i=x[n-i]\cdot2^F$ and weights $w_i$,
\begin{align}
a[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_{B_a}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{L-1} w_i\,x_i\Big), \label{eq:acc}\\
B_a &= 2W+\lceil\log_2 L\rceil, \label{eq:ba}\\
y[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_W\big(\operatorname{rs}(a[n],F)\big), \label{eq:yq}\\
e[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_W\big(d[n]-y[n]\big), \label{eq:ye}\\
w_i &\leftarrow \operatorname{sat}_W\big(w_i+\Delta w_i\big), \label{eq:wupd}
\end{align}
where the weight increments of LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift are
\begin{align}
\Delta w_i^{\text{LMS}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big((M_\mu\,e[n])\,x_i,\;F+10\big), \label{eq:dw_lms}\\
\Delta w_i^{\text{NLMS}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big(g[n]\,x_i,\;F\big), \label{eq:dw_nlms}\\
g[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_{W+4}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2N[n]+Q[n]}{2Q[n]}\right\rfloor\right), \label{eq:g}\\
\Delta w_i^{\text{Shift}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big(e[n]\,x_i,\;b(Q[n])-F+K\big), \label{eq:dw_shift}
\end{align}
with $\mu=M_\mu\,2^{-10}$, $N[n]=M_\mu\,e[n]\,2^{2F-10}$, $Q[n]=P[n]+2^{2F-E}$ for $\epsilon=2^{-E}$, and $b(\cdot)$ the
bit length of a positive integer. Every narrowing point saturates and sets a sticky flag, so an overflow
is never silent. The integer model (\texttt{py/fixedpoint.py}, \texttt{py/rtl\_model.py}) mirrors these
widths exactly and generates the golden vectors.

\section{Hardware architecture}\label{sec:hw}
\subsection{Core}\label{sec:core}
Fig.~\ref{fig:core} shows the datapath of the core (\texttt{rtl/anc\_core.v}, vendor-neutral Verilog). It
accepts one sample pair per \texttt{in\_valid} strobe and computes everything with a single multiplier,
pipelined in three stages (operand registers, product register, consume stage). A finite-state machine
steps through the phases of a sample: two cycles to square the new and the dropped reference sample
for \eqref{eq:prec} (NLMS and NLMS-Shift only), $L$ cycles for the filter sum \eqref{eq:acc}, one cycle for
$y$ and $e$ \eqref{eq:ye}, the step computation, and $L$ cycles of weight updates \eqref{eq:wupd}. NLMS adds
a sequential restoring divider for the gain $g[n]$; NLMS-Shift replaces it by a leading-one detector and a
shift-amount register. A \texttt{freeze} input holds the weights while filtering continues.

The schedule fixes the input-to-output latency and the number of busy cycles per sample:
\begin{align}
N_{\text{lat}} &= \begin{cases} L+3 & \text{LMS},\\ L+5 & \text{NLMS, NLMS-Shift},\end{cases} \label{eq:lat}\\
N_{\text{busy}} &= \begin{cases} 2L+9 & \text{LMS},\\ 2L+13+N_{\text{div}} & \text{NLMS},\\ 2L+8 & \text{NLMS-Shift},\end{cases} \label{eq:busy}
\end{align}
where the restoring divider runs one iteration per dividend bit, $N_{\text{div}}=W+B_\mu+2F+4+\lceil\log_2 L\rceil$,
and $B_\mu$ is the signed width of $M_\mu$, so the NLMS cycle count grows with the step-size constant. At
$L=32$ and the tuned settings this gives latencies of 35, 37 and 37 cycles and busy times of 73, 130
($N_{\text{div}}=53$) and 72 cycles, the values measured on the board (Section~\ref{sec:board}).

\subsection{Board wrapper}
The DE2-115 wrapper contains an I$^2$C engine that configures the WM8731 at reset (11 registers,
acknowledge-checked), an I$^2$S receiver and transmitter clocked by the 50~MHz system clock with the
codec's bit and word clocks sampled as data, two-flop synchronisers on the reset and switches, a
frame-latched bypass multiplexer, an overrun flag, and four registered test points on the GPIO header.
The codec runs as master from a 12.5~MHz clock with the sample-rate register set to 1536~fs, so
\begin{align}
f_s &= \frac{f_{\text{MCLK}}}{1536}=\frac{12.5~\text{MHz}}{1536}=8.138~\text{kHz}, \label{eq:fs}\\
N_s &= \frac{f_{\text{clk}}}{f_s}=\frac{50~\text{MHz}}{8.138~\text{kHz}}=6144, \label{eq:ns}
\end{align}
the rate of the simulation study, with $N_s$ clock cycles per sample. The core is busy for the fraction
\begin{equation}
U=\frac{N_{\text{busy}}}{N_s}
\label{eq:util}
\end{equation}
of each sample period, which by \eqref{eq:busy} is 1.19\%, 2.12\% and 1.17\% for LMS, NLMS and
NLMS-Shift. No processor or vendor IP is used.

\section{Experimental set-up}\label{sec:setup}
Synthetic scenarios follow the reference model $x=v$, $d=s+h*v+n_s$ at 8~kHz with 16,000 samples:
stationary coloured noise, amplitude steps ($\times3$ then $\times0.25$), an abrupt path switch at
the midpoint, and a recorded chord as the noise source. Step sizes and regularisers were selected on
seeds 101--103 by maximising the mean final noise reduction over stationary, amplitude-step and
path-switch scenarios with zero overflow: LMS $\mu=0.25$; NLMS $\mu=0.25$, $\epsilon=1$; NLMS-Shift
$K=3$, $\epsilon=0.25$. Results are reported on seeds 1--20. The final noise reduction is measured
over the last quarter $\mathcal{N}$ of the samples,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NR}=10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\big(d[n]-s[n]\big)^2}{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\big(e[n]-s[n]\big)^2}\ \text{dB},
\label{eq:nr}
\end{equation}
and each table entry is the mean over the $S=20$ seeds with its 95\% confidence interval,
\begin{equation}
\overline{\mathrm{NR}}\pm t_{0.975,\,S-1}\,\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{NR}}}{\sqrt{S}},\qquad t_{0.975,19}=2.093 .
\label{eq:ci}
\end{equation}
Two algorithms A and B are compared on the paired per-seed differences
$\Delta_k=\mathrm{NR}_{\mathrm{A},k}-\mathrm{NR}_{\mathrm{B},k}$. They are called equivalent when both one-sided
tests (TOST) reject, at $p<0.05$, that the mean difference lies outside the margin $\pm\delta$ with
$\delta=0.5$~dB:
\begin{equation}
H_{01}:\ \bar\Delta\le-\delta,\qquad H_{02}:\ \bar\Delta\ge\delta .
\label{eq:tost}
\end{equation}

For real recordings, four noise files (air conditioner, vacuum cleaner, copy machine, babble) and
ten utterances from the public MS-SNSD corpus\footnote{\url{https://github.com/microsoft/MS-SNSD}} were resampled to 8~kHz; each seed draws a noise
segment, an utterance and a random 16-tap acoustic path, at 0~dB input SNR, with and without
speech leakage into the reference at $-20$~dB. Noise reduction, the short-time objective intelligibility (STOI) measure and the narrow-band
perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ, ITU-T Recommendation P.862) are computed after a 2~s convergence window.

\subsection{Equivalent step size of NLMS-Shift}\label{sec:equiv}
By \eqref{eq:ratio}, the only difference between NLMS-Shift and NLMS is the factor $r[n]$. Averaged over
input levels spread uniformly on a logarithmic scale, $E[r]=\int_0^1 2^{u-1}\,du=1/(2\ln 2)\approx0.72$.
At a given level, however, $r$ is set by where $P+\epsilon$ lies within its octave: it grows with the level
and halves when $P+\epsilon$ crosses a power of two. With $L=32$ and $\epsilon=2^{-2}$, the crossing
$P+\epsilon=1/2$ lies at a reference RMS of $-21.1$~dBFS, 3~dB above the simulation level.
Fig.~\ref{fig:equiv} sweeps the input level over 12~dB in 0.75~dB steps in the integer model
(\texttt{py/exp\_equiv\_step.py}), on the stationary scenario (seeds 1--5) and on a two-tone reference. On
broadband noise the window power varies enough to smooth the sawtooth, and the mean ratio $\bar r$
moves gradually between 0.56 and 0.78. On the tonal reference $P$ is almost constant, and $\bar r$ falls
from 0.87 to 0.62 within 0.75~dB of level. In both cases NLMS run with the constant step $\bar r\,2^{-K}$
reproduces NLMS-Shift: the final noise reduction agrees within 0.27~dB at all 17 broadband levels and
within 0.55~dB at all tonal levels but the one that straddles the crossing (1.7~dB), and the convergence
time within 8\%. NLMS with the nominal step $2^{-K}$ differs by up to 3.5~dB and 48\%.

So the shift changes nothing in the adaptation but the step size: it turns the fixed NLMS step into a step between
$0.5$ and $1$ times the nominal one that depends on the input level. On broadband input this is a
smooth gain change that can be absorbed by the choice of $K$ (and $\epsilon$) for the expected level; on a
tonal input the step changes by up to a factor of about 0.7 when the level crosses a power-of-two
boundary of $P+\epsilon$, which a fixed-step NLMS does not do.

\subsection{Gate-level power and energy per sample}\label{sec:power}
To obtain activity-based figures, the core alone (\texttt{anc\_core}, without the codec interface) was
compiled for each algorithm, and its post-fit netlist was simulated in Questa with SDF back-annotation
at the slow 1.2~V, 85\,$^\circ$C corner. A netlist-only testbench feeds the same golden input vectors
that the RTL regression uses and checks every output against the integer model; the recorded switching
activity (VCD) is then read by the Quartus Power Analyzer. Each core is run in two modes. In the
\emph{board} mode a new sample arrives every $G=6{,}144$ clock cycles (200 samples), as on the board.
In the \emph{max} mode samples arrive back to back, a few cycles above the busy cycles of each core
($G=81$, 138 and 80 for LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift, 2,000 samples). With $f_{\mathrm{clk}}=50$~MHz the
sample rate and the dynamic energy per sample are
\begin{align}
f_s &= \frac{f_{\mathrm{clk}}}{G}, \label{eq:fs}\\
E_s &= \frac{P_{\mathrm{dyn}}}{f_s} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{dyn}}\,G}{f_{\mathrm{clk}}}, \label{eq:es}
\end{align}
where $P_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ is the core dynamic power reported by the Power Analyzer. Static and I/O power
are left out of $E_s$ because they do not depend on the algorithm (Table~\ref{tab:power}).

All six netlist simulations reproduce every golden output, so the activity behind each number is that of
a correct computation. Core static power (99.5--99.7~mW) and I/O power (52--53~mW) are almost equal in
all runs, so only the core dynamic power separates the algorithms. At the board rate LMS needs
3.90~mW, NLMS-Shift 5.93~mW and NLMS 7.63~mW; NLMS-Shift uses 22\% less dynamic energy per sample
than NLMS (728.7 against 937.6~nJ). At the maximum rate the ranking of energy per sample is the same,
LMS 20.7~nJ, NLMS-Shift 28.4~nJ and NLMS 38.0~nJ, a 25\% saving of NLMS-Shift over NLMS, although
NLMS-Shift draws the highest power there because it processes 1.7 times as many samples per second.

The two modes also show where the energy goes. Taking the dynamic power as linear in the sample rate,
\begin{align}
P_{\mathrm{dyn}}(f_s) &= P_0 + E_c\,f_s, \label{eq:plin}\\
E_c &= \frac{P_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\max}-P_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\mathrm{board}}}{f_s^{\max}-f_s^{\mathrm{board}}},
\qquad P_0 = P_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\mathrm{board}} - E_c\,f_s^{\mathrm{board}}, \label{eq:p0}
\end{align}
the two runs of each core give a rate-independent part $P_0$ of 3.78, 7.49 and 5.77~mW and a
per-sample compute energy $E_c$ of 14.6, 17.4 and 19.2~nJ for LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift. $P_0$ is the
clock tree and the registers that are clocked every cycle whether or not a sample is being processed;
it follows the register count of the core (960, 1,241 and 1,022). At 8.138~kHz it accounts for more
than 97\% of the dynamic energy per sample of every core, which is why the board-rate values are so
much larger than the max-rate ones. The saving of NLMS-Shift over NLMS therefore comes from its smaller
always-on state and from finishing a sample in 72 instead of 130 busy cycles, not from cheaper
arithmetic: its compute energy per sample is about 10\% higher than that of NLMS, as the wider update
path and the shifter switch more nets. A two-point fit is a simple model, so $P_0$ and $E_c$ should be
read as a split of the measured totals rather than as separate measurements.

Huang and Yao~\cite{huang2026}, on the same board, report 5.07~nJ per sample for their binary-reciprocal
NLMS IIR filter against 8.33~nJ with an SRT divider, a 39.1\% reduction. Replacing the restoring
divider by the shift in ULLAP reduces the energy per sample at the maximum rate by 25\%, the same
direction. The absolute values are not comparable: their design is fully parallel and runs at
megasamples per second, while ULLAP shares one multiplier over 72 to 130 cycles, so most of its energy
is clocking spent while the datapath steps through the taps. Two details of the flow are reported for
reproducibility: \texttt{quartus\_pow} needs an Assembler run, which the build script now includes, and
the NLMS-Shift netlist reports three hold-time checks at 73~ns, where the testbench releases the reset on
a clock edge; this is before the activity recording starts, and all outputs match.



\subsection{On-board results}\label{sec:board}
The board evidence is recorded by the FPGA itself with Signal Tap, so it does not depend on an
external recorder or oscilloscope. One logic-analyser instance stores, once per sample, the two
codec samples entering the core ($x$ and $d$, register outputs \texttt{x\_reg} and \texttt{d\_reg}) and
the core output $e$, with a transitional storage qualifier on \texttt{out\_valid}, and triggers when the
reset button is released; it holds 32,768 samples (4.0~s). A second instance samples the four GPIO
test points at 50~MHz. The stimuli are the noise-only versions of the four scenarios, played from a
laptop into LINE IN after a 1~kHz calibration tone; the reset is pressed during the tone, so each
capture starts from zero weights on real codec data at about $-25$~dBFS, and no sample in any capture
reaches full scale. The analysis script and the raw exports are in the repository
(\texttt{py/analyze\_board\_capture.py}, \texttt{board\_evidence/}).

\emph{Bit-exactness.} For each of the 12 captures (three algorithms, four scenarios) the integer model
of Section~\ref{sec:model}, started from zero state at the first sample after the reset and fed the
captured $x$ and $d$, reproduces every captured $e[n]$: 351,723 samples in total, 28,672 to 32,768
(3.5 to 4.0~s) per capture, with no mismatch. The fixed-point arithmetic of the silicon is therefore
exactly the one evaluated in simulation, also on analogue input with the codec's noise and offsets.

\emph{Timing.} The GPIO capture gives an \texttt{in\_valid} period of 6,144 clock cycles (8,138.02~Hz),
an \texttt{in\_valid} to \texttt{out\_valid} spacing of 38, 36 and 38 cycles for NLMS-Shift, LMS and
NLMS (the core latency plus one accept cycle, 0.76, 0.72 and 0.76~$\mu$s) and busy times of 72, 73 and
130 cycles, all equal to the RTL simulation of the same parameters. The Signal Tap builds still meet
the 50~MHz constraint (setup slack $+2.65$, $+5.68$ and $+3.78$~ns); Table~\ref{tab:hw} is for the builds
without the logic analyser.

Fig.~\ref{fig:board} shows the three algorithms on the same amplitude-step stimulus, aligned to the start of
the scenario (KEY0 was pressed by hand during the calibration tone, so the reset falls at a slightly
different point of the stimulus in each capture). All three settle to about 15~dB in the first 1.5~s
and track the step at 2.1~s, where the level of $d$ rises by 7.4~dB, with only a short burst in $e$:
the residual power after the step ($-47$ to $-52$~dBFS) is below its level before it ($-44$~dBFS), so
the noise reduction rises to 25 to 30~dB. Over the last quarter of the window the reduction is about
40~dB below 1.5~kHz and 11 to 15~dB near the band edge (3.5 to 3.9~kHz).

\emph{Noise reduction on the simulation signal.} In a second session the stimulus was the 2~s,
seed-1 noise-only signal of each scenario, i.e.\ exactly the 16,000-sample signal of the simulations,
played once after the calibration tone so that the whole scenario lies inside the capture. Its start is
found by correlating the captured $x$ with the stimulus (correlation 0.95 to 0.99), and
$\mathrm{NR}_\mathrm{final}$ is taken over the same last 25~\% as in simulation. All 12 captures (28,672
samples after the reset each) are again bit-exact. Table~\ref{tab:board} compares the board with the
integer model on the same signal. The board is 2.9 to 10.6~dB below the simulation, by similar amounts for all three
algorithms, and the cause is the input level, not the hardware: the codec delivered $x$ and $d$ at
about $-27$~dBFS, 3~dB below the simulation level, and at the tuned step sizes a lower input power slows
adaptation within the 2~s. Run at the level the board received, the model reproduces the board within
0.2~dB on stationary and path-switch noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:boardlevel}). For amplitude steps and the
recorded noise, whose final quarter is quiet ($d$ at about $-41$ and $-57$~dBFS), the board stays 1.7 to
8.1~dB below the level-matched model; there the analogue noise of the playback and codec path
($-70$~dBFS measured in the silence after the stimulus) and an ADC offset that decays during the first
second after the reset are no longer small against $d$, and for amplitude steps the best 32-tap least-squares filter fitted to the board samples of that
window reaches 24~dB instead of 28~dB on the ideal signal. On the board the three algorithms are
within 0.7~dB of one another in every scenario.

\emph{Freeze, DAC word and endurance.} With NLMS-Shift on stationary noise, SW[1] was flipped by hand
1.44~s into the scenario. The captured output equals the model with adaptation stopped from that
sample on (28,672 samples, no mismatch), and the noise reduction stays at 15.2~dB from the switch to
the end of the scenario, against 15.4~dB just before it and 15.9~dB had adaptation continued. A third
Signal Tap instance recorded the word sent to the DAC: in ANC mode it equals $\{e, 0000\}$ and in bypass
($\mathrm{SW}[0]=1$) its upper 12 bits equal $d$, in all 1,792 samples of each capture. Since the LINE OUT
signal could not be recorded with the available equipment, this closes the chain up to the DAC
interface. After 10~minutes of continuous operation on looped noise, the GPIO timing was unchanged
(6,144 cycles per sample, 38 cycles from \texttt{in\_valid} to \texttt{out\_valid}, 72 busy cycles) and
no saturation, NACK or overrun flag was lit; the build with all three logic-analyser instances still
met timing (slack $+2.93$~ns).

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:disc}
The comparison supports a narrow conclusion: the divider-free normalisation keeps NLMS's
level-independence at about LMS's cycle count, so it is the better choice when the noise level
changes and throughput or energy per sample matters, while LMS remains the smallest when the level is
steady. It does not show that NLMS-Shift is better than NLMS in cancellation. Section~\ref{sec:equiv} explains why: the shift
changes only the step size, by a level-dependent factor between 0.5 and 1, so the choice of $K$ and
$\epsilon$ for the expected input level matters more than the shift itself, and tonal noise near a
power-of-two boundary of $P+\epsilon$ sees a step jump that NLMS does not have. On real recordings, the
speech at the primary input limits all three algorithms far more than the algorithm choice does
(6.4--17.9~dB against at most 0.6~dB between algorithms).
The acoustic paths are simulated, also for the real recordings; the evaluation does not cover
acoustic feedback (closed-loop howling), for which reference-based ANC
is not the right structure. The power figures are for the core alone; the codec interface and status
logic of the board design add to them, and the energy split of \eqref{eq:plin} rests on two operating points.
On the board, the output is proven equal to the model up to the DAC word, but the analogue LINE OUT
signal was not recorded, and the absolute noise reduction depends on the input level: with the
stimulus 3~dB below the simulation level the board reached 14 to 17~dB instead of 19 to 28~dB on the
synthetic scenarios, and on stationary and path-switch noise the model reproduces this at that level. A deployment should therefore set the
codec input gain so that the reference uses most of the 12-bit range, or retune the step size for the
expected level.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
ULLAP implements LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift on one serial 12-bit, 32-tap core on a low-cost Cyclone IV
board, compared under equal tuning effort. NLMS-Shift matches NLMS within the 95\% confidence interval
on stationary and amplitude-step noise while needing 72 instead of 130 busy cycles per sample, and all
three remove 11.7 to 14.9~dB of real recorded noise, or 18.5 to 31.9~dB when the speech is
removed from the primary input, which shows that the speech, not the noise, limits the result. NLMS-Shift
equals NLMS with a level-dependent step between 0.5 and 1 times the nominal one. On the board, 25 Signal Tap captures of real codec
data (724,459 samples) are bit-exact with the integer model, as are the freeze behaviour and the DAC
word, and on stationary and path-switch noise the measured noise reduction equals the model's at the
input level the board received within 0.2~dB.
