\section{Introduction}
Adaptive noise cancellation (ANC) estimates the noise that reaches a primary sensor from a
separate reference sensor and subtracts it~\cite{widrow1975}. The LMS algorithm and its normalised
form (NLMS) remain the standard choice for this task because of their low cost and robustness.
NLMS makes the adaptation speed independent of the input level, but it needs a division per
sample, which is expensive in hardware. Replacing that division by a power-of-two approximation
of the input power is an old idea~\cite{garrett2002}, and quantised-normaliser and lookup-table
reciprocals have been proposed since~\cite{zhao2018,huang2024}.

Two questions are left open by the FPGA literature on these filters. First, variants are usually
compared with different tuning effort, so a reported advantage can come from an under-tuned
baseline; this happened in an earlier version of this work, where a 5.7~dB advantage of the
divider-free variant over NLMS disappeared after NLMS was regularised fairly. Second, hardware
results are rarely tied to the reference model: resource and timing reports show that a design
fits, not that it computes what the simulation claims.

This paper addresses both on a low-cost platform and makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A single serial core that implements LMS, NLMS (restoring divider) and NLMS-Shift with one
shared multiplier, so the three are compared on identical datapath, word length and filter length.
\item A fair comparison: every algorithm's step size and regulariser are chosen on held-out seeds
with the same criterion, and results are reported with 20-seed 95\% confidence intervals,
on synthetic scenarios and on real recorded noise and speech.
\item A bit-exact verification chain from a Python integer model to the RTL and the board wrapper
(86 regression tests including codec, freeze and reset behaviour), and on-board captures of real codec
samples in which every output sample of all three algorithms equals the model's.
\item A reproducible word-length study and a complete processor-free audio system that closes
timing at 50~MHz on Cyclone IV E, released with all scripts and data.
\end{enumerate}
The algorithmic idea behind NLMS-Shift is not claimed as new; Section~\ref{sec:related} positions it.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
Garrett et al.~\cite{garrett2002} replaced the NLMS division in a decision-feedback equaliser by
power-of-two operations obtained from leading-zero counts of the error and the filter energy;
NLMS-Shift is the real-valued, error-exact special case of that approach. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2018}
quantised the normaliser of a variable-regularised LMS beamformer to a few levels implemented with
canonical signed digits and report at least 21\% fewer LUTs and 26.85\% higher throughput than a
divider design on Virtex-7 (22-bit words, eight sensors). Huang et al.~\cite{huang2024} replaced
the divider by a 6-bit-input, 4-bit-output reciprocal table; on the same DE2-115 board used here,
their 16-tap, 16-bit fully parallel NLMS FIR filter uses 11,276 logic elements, runs at 91~MHz with
11 cycles per sample, and reaches 10.52~nJ per sample against 18.60~nJ for a 4-bit SRT divider.
Huang and Yao~\cite{huang2026} proposed a binary reciprocal approximation built from a leading-one
detector and a barrel shifter for a fifth-order NLMS IIR filter, again on the DE2-115, and report
5.07~nJ per sample against 8.33~nJ for a 15-bit SRT divider. The base work of this
project~\cite{rodrigo2024} simulated an LMS feedback suppressor in MATLAB and Vivado without
numeric results.
None of these works compares LMS, a true divider and a shift normaliser on one core with equal tuning
and statistical tests, and few test a complete audio system with a codec on a board.
Compared with these, ULLAP's contribution is the equal-effort three-way comparison on one core,
the bit-exact chain to hardware, and the evaluation on real recordings.

\section{Algorithms and fixed-point model}\label{sec:model}
With reference vector $\mathbf{x}[n]=[x[n],\dots,x[n-L+1]]^T$, weights $\mathbf{w}[n]$ and primary
input $d[n]$, all three algorithms compute
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
y[n]=\mathbf{w}^T[n]\mathbf{x}[n],\qquad e[n]=d[n]-y[n],\\
\mathbf{w}[n+1]=\mathbf{w}[n]+\mu[n]\,e[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n],
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with $\mu[n]=\mu$ for LMS and $\mu[n]=\mu/(P[n]+\epsilon)$, $P[n]=\|\mathbf{x}[n]\|^2$, for NLMS.
NLMS-Shift replaces the division by a shift:
\begin{equation}
\mu[n]=2^{-(\lfloor\log_2(P[n]+\epsilon)\rfloor+1+K)},
\end{equation}
so that $\mu/(2(P+\epsilon))<\mu[n]\le\mu/(P+\epsilon)$ with $\mu=2^{-K}$: the effective step lies
within a factor of two below the NLMS step, which keeps the NLMS stability bound. In hardware the
exponent is the position of the leading one of $P[n]+\epsilon$, found by a priority encoder, and the
update is a barrel shift of $e[n]x[n-i]$.

All signals and weights are signed $Q1.(W-1)$ with $W=12$; the accumulator has $2W+\lceil\log_2L\rceil$
bits; every narrowing point saturates and raises a sticky flag; rounding is to nearest. The power
$P[n]$ is updated incrementally by adding $x^2[n]$ and subtracting $x^2[n-L]$. The integer model
(\texttt{py/fixedpoint.py}, \texttt{py/rtl\_model.py}) mirrors these widths exactly and generates the
golden vectors.

\section{Hardware architecture}
\subsection{Core}
The core (\texttt{rtl/anc\_core.v}, vendor-neutral Verilog) processes one sample per \texttt{in\_valid}
strobe with a single multiplier pipelined in three stages: $L$ cycles for the filter sum, then the
error, then $L$ cycles of weight updates. NLMS adds a sequential restoring divider for the gain;
NLMS-Shift replaces it by a leading-one detector and a shift-amount register. A \texttt{freeze} input
holds the weights while filtering continues. The input-to-output latency is $L+3$ cycles for LMS and
$L+5$ for NLMS and NLMS-Shift (35 and 37 cycles at $L=32$), and at the tuned settings the core is busy
for 73, 130 and 72 cycles per sample respectively; the NLMS divider runs one iteration per dividend bit,
and the dividend widens with the step-size constant, so its cycle count depends on $\mu$.

\subsection{Board wrapper}
The DE2-115 wrapper contains an I$^2$C engine that configures the WM8731 at reset (11 registers,
acknowledge-checked), an I$^2$S receiver and transmitter clocked by the 50~MHz system clock with the
codec's bit and word clocks sampled as data, two-flop synchronisers on the reset and switches, a
frame-latched bypass multiplexer, an overrun flag, and four registered test points on the GPIO header.
The codec runs as master from a 12.5~MHz clock with the sample-rate register set to 1536~fs, giving
8.138~kHz, the rate of the simulation study. No processor or vendor IP is used.

\section{Experimental set-up}
Synthetic scenarios follow the reference model $x=v$, $d=s+h*v+n_s$ at 8~kHz with 16,000 samples:
stationary coloured noise, amplitude steps ($\times3$ then $\times0.25$), an abrupt path switch at
the midpoint, and a recorded chord as the noise source. Step sizes and regularisers were selected on
seeds 101--103 by maximising the mean final noise reduction over stationary, amplitude-step and
path-switch scenarios with zero overflow: LMS $\mu=0.25$; NLMS $\mu=0.25$, $\epsilon=1$; NLMS-Shift
$K=3$, $\epsilon=0.25$. Results are reported on seeds 1--20. The final noise reduction is
$\mathrm{NR}=10\log_{10}\sum(d-s)^2/\sum(e-s)^2$ over the last 25\% of samples.

For real recordings, four noise files (air conditioner, vacuum cleaner, copy machine, babble) and
ten utterances from MS-SNSD~\cite{reddy2019} were resampled to 8~kHz; each seed draws a noise
segment, an utterance and a random 16-tap acoustic path, at 0~dB input SNR, with and without
speech leakage into the reference at $-20$~dB. Noise reduction, STOI~\cite{taal2011} and narrow-band
PESQ~\cite{pesq} are computed after a 2~s convergence window.

Table~\ref{tab:real} shows that on all four real noises the filter roughly doubles PESQ and brings STOI
close to 1, and that the order of the algorithms is the same everywhere: NLMS 0.4--0.6~dB ahead of LMS,
NLMS-Shift 0.15--0.35~dB behind NLMS. Real noise is less predictable than the coloured synthetic noise,
so the noise reduction is 8--11~dB lower than in Table~\ref{tab:nr}. When $-20$~dB of the speech leaks into
the reference, the filter also partly cancels speech: noise reduction drops by 2.3--4.6~dB, PESQ by
0.25--0.6, and saturation events appear (18,500 over 19.2 million processed samples, most for the air
conditioner), so reference-microphone placement matters more than the choice of algorithm
(\texttt{paper/tab\_real\_audio\_xt.tex}).

\subsection{Word length}
At the tuned settings, the worst-scenario loss relative to the floating-point run of the same
algorithm is 4.1--5.9~dB at $W=10$, 0.9--1.2~dB at $W=11$ and 0.3--0.4~dB at $W=12$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:wl}); 11 bits is the shortest word within the 3~dB criterion and 12 bits was kept for
margin. On the tonal chord recording the fixed-point filter outperforms floating point by up to
3.4~dB at $W=11$ and loses 1.3~dB at $W=13$: on this narrow-band signal quantisation changes the
effective adaptation, so the chord result is sensitive to the word length and should not decide it.

LMS is about 31\% smaller than either normalised variant. NLMS-Shift and NLMS use about the same logic
(2,610 and 2,626 logic elements), because the divider's area is replaced by the leading-one detector,
shifter and wider update path, but NLMS-Shift has 15\% fewer registers, needs 45\% fewer busy cycles
per sample and toggles 16\% fewer bits per sample. All three designs meet the 50~MHz constraint with at
least +2.953~ns setup slack and +0.401~ns hold slack, with no critical warnings. At 8.138~kHz the busiest
variant uses 2\% of the 6,144-cycle sample period, so a single core could serve many channels or much
longer filters.

The power figures are Quartus vectorless estimates with the default 12.5\% toggle rate, which Quartus
itself rates as low confidence. Core static power (about 98.7~mW) dominates the 169 to 173~mW total.
The dynamic estimates rank the designs LMS $<$ NLMS-Shift $<$ NLMS, as the toggle counts do, but a
fixed toggle rate mostly tracks area and ignores that the datapath is idle for 98\% of each sample
period, so these numbers are upper bounds and not energy per sample.
A gate-level, activity-annotated estimate is left for future work: the post-fit netlist flattens the
hierarchy that the top-level testbench observes, so it needs a netlist-only testbench.
For comparison with~\cite{huang2024,huang2026}, which use the same board, energy per sample should be
reported as dynamic power divided by the sample rate from an activity-annotated Power Analyzer run,
which this work does not yet have, so no energy comparison with them is claimed. Their designs
are fully parallel and run at megasamples per second, while ULLAP shares one multiplier and runs at
8.138~kHz, so area and energy are compared per tap and per sample, not as raw totals.



\subsection{On-board results}
The board evidence is recorded by the FPGA itself with Signal Tap, so it does not depend on an
external recorder or oscilloscope. One logic-analyser instance stores, once per sample, the two
codec samples entering the core ($x$ and $d$, register outputs \texttt{x\_reg} and \texttt{d\_reg}) and
the core output $e$, with a transitional storage qualifier on \texttt{out\_valid}, and triggers when the
reset button is released; it holds 32,768 samples (4.0~s). A second instance samples the four GPIO
test points at 50~MHz. The stimuli are the noise-only versions of the four scenarios, played from a
laptop into LINE IN after a 1~kHz calibration tone; the reset is pressed during the tone, so each
capture starts from zero weights on real codec data at about $-25$~dBFS, and no sample in any capture
reaches full scale. The analysis script and the raw exports are in the repository
(\texttt{py/analyze\_board\_capture.py}, \texttt{board\_evidence/}).

\emph{Bit-exactness.} For each of the 12 captures (three algorithms, four scenarios) the integer model
of Section~\ref{sec:model}, started from zero state at the first sample after the reset and fed the
captured $x$ and $d$, reproduces every captured $e[n]$: 351,723 samples in total, 28,672 to 32,768
(3.5 to 4.0~s) per capture, with no mismatch. The fixed-point arithmetic of the silicon is therefore
exactly the one evaluated in simulation, also on analogue input with the codec's noise and offsets.

\emph{Timing.} The GPIO capture gives an \texttt{in\_valid} period of 6,144 clock cycles (8,138.02~Hz),
an \texttt{in\_valid} to \texttt{out\_valid} spacing of 38, 36 and 38 cycles for NLMS-Shift, LMS and
NLMS (the core latency plus one accept cycle, 0.76, 0.72 and 0.76~$\mu$s) and busy times of 72, 73 and
130 cycles, all equal to the RTL simulation of the same parameters. The Signal Tap builds still meet
the 50~MHz constraint (setup slack $+2.65$, $+5.68$ and $+3.78$~ns); Table~\ref{tab:hw} is for the builds
without the logic analyser.

Fig.~\ref{fig:board} shows one capture: after the tone, the residual falls within about 0.5~s, and when
the noise level triples at 2.35~s the normalised filter recovers within a few hundred milliseconds.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
The comparison supports a narrow conclusion: the divider-free normalisation keeps NLMS's
level-independence at about LMS's cycle count, so it is the better choice when the noise level
changes and throughput or energy per sample matters, while LMS remains the smallest when the level is
steady. It does not show that NLMS-Shift is better than NLMS in cancellation.
The acoustic paths are simulated, also for the real recordings; the evaluation does not cover
acoustic feedback (the closed-loop howling scenario of the base work), for which reference-based ANC
is not the right structure. Power is a switching-activity proxy until gate-level analysis is done.
