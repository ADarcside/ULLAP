\section{Introduction}
Noise is present in almost every place where people live and work, such as the hum of an office air
conditioner, a compressor near a production line, road noise inside a vehicle cabin and the background
chatter around a hearing-aid user. When a second microphone can be placed close to the noise source, the
noise that reaches the main microphone can be predicted from this reference and subtracted from it. The
predictor used for this purpose is an adaptive filter, which learns the acoustic path between the two
microphones during operation and tracks the path whenever it changes. The least-mean-square (LMS)
algorithm and its normalized form (NLMS) are still the most widely used algorithms for this filter, since
they need only a few multiply--accumulate operations per tap and per sample, and their behavior has been
analyzed for several decades~\cite{lu2021a}.

In a small embedded device, this filter must operate in real time with a short and fixed delay and with
very low energy consumption. A field-programmable gate array (FPGA) is well suited to this requirement,
because the datapath is built around the algorithm, the latency is known to the exact clock cycle, and no
processor or operating system is required for scheduling. However, LMS and NLMS are not equally simple to
realize in hardware. LMS needs only multipliers and adders, but its convergence speed depends on the level
of the input signal. NLMS removes this dependence by dividing the step size by the input power, which
requires one division in every sample. Since dividers are slow and occupy a large amount of logic, recent
designs approximate the division instead, using an approximate Newton--Raphson
iteration~\cite{guidotti2020}, canonical-signed-digit reciprocals~\cite{yao2024}, or a binary reciprocal
formed from a leading-one detector and a shifter~\cite{huang2026}. The simplest option rounds the input
power to a power of two, so that the division reduces to a plain shift. In this work, this variant is
referred to as NLMS-Shift.

Two issues in the existing literature motivate this work. The first issue is tuning. The compared
variants are rarely tuned with the same care, and hence a reported gain may come from a weak baseline
rather than from the algorithm itself. An early version of this work suffered from the same problem:
NLMS-Shift appeared 5.7~dB better than NLMS, and the gap disappeared once NLMS was given a suitable
regularization constant. The second issue is the link between the model and the silicon. Hardware papers
generally report area, clock rate and power. These figures prove that a design fits on the chip, but they
do not prove that the chip produces the same samples as the model from which the accuracy results were
obtained.

In this paper, ULLAP, a 12-bit, 32-tap adaptive noise canceller, is presented for the low-cost Terasic
DE2-115 board (Intel Cyclone IV E) with its WM8731 audio codec, and it is used to address both issues. The
major contributions of this work are given below.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Single serial core for three algorithms:} A single serial core is designed to run LMS, NLMS
(with a restoring divider) and NLMS-Shift on one shared multiplier, so that the three algorithms share the
same datapath, word length and filter length. The core operates inside a complete processor-free audio
system, closes timing at 50~MHz on Cyclone IV E, and is released along with all scripts and data.
\item \textbf{Fair comparison:} The step size and the regularizer of each algorithm are selected on
held-out seeds using the same rule, and every result is reported with a 20-seed 95\% confidence interval.
The comparison covers synthetic scenarios as well as real recorded noise and speech, and includes a
reproducible word-length study.
\item \textbf{Bit-exact verification chain:} A bit-exact verification chain is built from a Python integer
model through the RTL to the board wrapper (86 regression tests, including codec, freeze and reset
behavior). On the board, captures of real codec samples show that every output sample of all three
algorithms is equal to that of the model.
\item \textbf{Two measured explanations:} The first explanation concerns the shift. NLMS-Shift is shown to
be NLMS with a step that varies between 0.5 and 1 times the nominal step as the input level changes. NLMS
operated with the average of this step reproduces NLMS-Shift within 0.3~dB on broadband noise, whereas on
tonal input the step jumps whenever the window power crosses a power of two. The second explanation
concerns the real recordings. When the speech is removed, the same filters remove 18.5--31.9~dB of the
same real noise. Hence, the lower noise reduction obtained with speech is caused by the speech disturbing
the adaptation and not by the noise.
\end{enumerate}
The shift normalizer itself is not new and is not claimed as a contribution; Section~\ref{sec:related}
places it among the earlier division-free designs.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the problem and the
related works. Section~\ref{sec:system} gives an overview of the system, Section~\ref{sec:model}
describes the algorithms and the fixed-point model, and Section~\ref{sec:hw} explains the hardware

and~\ref{sec:concl} give the discussion and the concluding remarks.

\section{Literature review}\label{sec:related}
\subsection{The problem: noise in daily life}
At present, noise is regarded as an environmental health risk and not merely as a nuisance. In Europe,
more than 113 million people are exposed to transport noise above 55~dB(A), and traffic noise alone is
estimated to cause the loss of about 1.3 million healthy life-years every year through its effect on the
heart and blood vessels~\cite{munzel2025}. Hahad et al.~\cite{hahad2025} reviewed the evidence that
long-term noise exposure is also associated with mental-health disorders and discussed the biological
mechanisms that may explain this association. Hearing loss forms the other side of the problem. According
to the World Health Organization, about 1.5 billion people live with some degree of hearing loss, and this
number is expected to reach 2.5 billion by 2050~\cite{chadha2021}. For these listeners, background noise
is the main reason why speech remains difficult to follow, even with a hearing aid. Reducing the noise at
the microphone or near the ear is therefore useful in many settings, such as beside roads, where urban
traffic noise lies mostly below 2~kHz~\cite{yang2024}, inside vehicle cabins~\cite{jiang2023,zhu2024},
in ventilation ducts and air conditioners~\cite{zhu2025}, and in hearing
aids~\cite{yin2023,husstedt2023,tetteh2026}. Many of these devices are small, inexpensive and
battery-powered, and hence the processing must run in real time at low cost and low energy.

\subsection{Applications of adaptive noise control}
Yang et al.~\cite{yang2024} recorded road traffic noise at five sites in Beijing and developed a
feedforward LMS prototype, which removed close to 30~dB(A) of single- and dual-frequency noise below
2~kHz. Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang2023} presented an in-car FxLMS system in which the step size depends on
the reference norm, and the two parameters of this rule were tuned by particle-swarm optimization. On a
dSPACE board, this system achieved up to 12~dB below 100~Hz and about 3~dB over 20--400~Hz in road tests,
whereas plain FxLMS obtained between $-0.8$ and 0.3~dB. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2024} used the vehicle's own
loudspeakers to control the first four engine orders. This system attained an average reduction of
6.2~dB at 52 ear-level microphones, and in a jury test 93\% of the listeners felt more comfortable with
the system switched on. For hearing aids, Yin and Chen~\cite{yin2023} introduced a weighted error into a
variable-step adaptive feedback canceller, which converged 35\% faster than a PEM-IMLMS baseline and 60\%
faster than PEM-NLMS on a real device. Husstedt et al.~\cite{husstedt2023} evaluated six commercial
behind-the-ear devices and observed that impulse noise reduction lowers the C-weighted peak levels by up
to 20~dB, or by about 9~dB on average. A recent systematic review of speech enhancement for hearing
aids~\cite{tetteh2026} reported that deep networks perform best in complex acoustic scenes, while hybrid
methods that retain an adaptive filter provide a better balance between latency and computation on
wearable devices.

\subsection{Adaptive algorithms}
Lu et al.\ surveyed a decade of active noise control algorithms in two parts, the first on linear
systems~\cite{lu2021a} and the second on nonlinear systems~\cite{lu2021b}. In both parts, LMS-type updates
remain the base on which most of the newer methods are built. A popular approach is to run two filters at
the same time, one fast and one accurate, and to combine their outputs. F\'elix et al.~\cite{felix2021}
proposed a modified convex combination of FXLMS filters, which converged faster and attenuated more than
FXLMS and the earlier convex scheme. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2025} combined an adaptive FxLMS filter and a
CNN--GRU network through a convex weight for cancelling harmonic noise in a bent air-conditioner pipe. A
different problem occurs when the loudspeaker output leaks back into the reference microphone and the
loop becomes unstable. Li et al.~\cite{li2024} addressed this problem with a Youla-parameterized
feedforward controller and an adaptive IIR update, which was tested on a duct. All these methods improve
the cancellation, but they add extra operations, networks or complete filters to every sample, which is
contrary to the requirements of a small FPGA design.

\subsection{Hardware realizations}
In terms of hardware, Thannoon and Hashim~\cite{thannoon2024} combined a fast retimed RLS filter and a
slow LMS filter on an FPGA to remove power-line interference from ECG records of the MIT-BIH database.
This design achieved 24.2\% higher SNR and 50\% faster convergence than conventional structures.
Jagadeeswari et al.~\cite{jagadeeswari2026} applied cut-set retiming to delayed NLMS filters for audio on
a Spartan-6 device and reduced the critical path from 29.96 to 13.39~ns. For underwater sonar, Karakaya
and Aytar~\cite{karakaya2026} implemented semi-parallel 16-bit NLMS and variable-step LMS filters on an
Artix-7 device, which raised the SNR from 8.85~dB to 29.36 and 28.57~dB using 2.51\% and 2.91\% of the
LUTs, respectively. Park and Park~\cite{park2023} followed a different approach, in which a small CNN
classifies the noise and selects one of several fixed filters; in simulation, this method obtained 2.3~dB
more SNR than LMS with 53\% less dynamic power. Abdi et al.~\cite{abdi2024} developed a narrowband
controller around a variable-step LMS notch filter on an FPGA to suppress microphonics in a
superconducting accelerator cavity. Other works focused on the arithmetic itself. Nagabushanam et
al.~\cite{nagabushanam2025} designed a multiplier-less LMS filter using distributed arithmetic with
offset-binary coding, and da Rosa et al.~\cite{rosa2022} replaced the multipliers of an LMS power-line
canceller with adders and shifters, which saved up to 4.98 times in power and 30.46\% in area.

\subsection{Removing the division from NLMS}
The works closest to the present study are those that remove the NLMS division directly. Guidotti et
al.~\cite{guidotti2020} reduced the power of an NLMS interference canceller with an approximate
Newton--Raphson integer divider, and Coelho et al.~\cite{coelho2022} lowered the cost of adaptive
filtering using sum-of-powers-of-two arithmetic. Yao and Huang~\cite{yao2024} approximated the reciprocal
of the input power with canonical-signed-digit encoded divisors in a 16-tap NLMS FIR filter. Later, Huang
and Yao~\cite{huang2026} proposed a binary reciprocal approximation for an NLMS IIR filter. On the same
DE2-115 board used in this work, their design consumed 5.07~nJ per sample against 8.33~nJ with a 15-bit
SRT divider, and it reduced the energy per sample by 39.1\%, 6.5\%, 14.4\% and 5.6\% relative to the SRT,
Newton--Raphson, CSD and lookup-table methods~\cite{huang2026}. The same authors also noted that the older
idea of rounding the divisor to a single power of two, which turns the division into a shift, has a large
approximation error. This plain shift is exactly NLMS-Shift, and Section~\ref{sec:equiv} shows that its
error acts only as a level-dependent step factor between 0.5 and 1.

From the above review, no work was found that compares LMS, a true divider and a shift normalizer on
one core with equal tuning and statistical tests, and only a few works test a complete audio system with
a codec on a board. ULLAP adds this equal-effort three-way comparison, a bit-exact chain from the model
to the hardware, and an evaluation on real recordings.

\section{System overview}\label{sec:system}
The overall system is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:system}. The noise from the source $v$ travels to the
primary microphone through an unknown acoustic path $h$ and is mixed there with the desired signal $s$. A
reference microphone placed near the source picks up the noise $v$ itself. The two microphones are
connected to the left and right LINE IN channels of the WM8731 codec, which digitizes the signals and
passes them to the FPGA over an I$^2$S link. Inside the FPGA, the I$^2$S receiver delivers one pair
$(x[n],d[n])$ in every sample period to the ANC core. The core returns the error $e[n]$, which is the
cleaned signal, and the I$^2$S transmitter sends this signal back to the codec DAC and LINE OUT. An
I$^2$C engine programs the codec once after reset. Two slide switches select the bypass mode and freeze
the adaptation, a push button resets the system, and four GPIO test points bring out the sample strobe,
the output strobe, the busy flag and the frame clock for timing measurement. The complete design is
written in plain Verilog, without any processor or vendor IP.

\section{Algorithms and fixed-point model}\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Signal model and cost function}
The reference microphone receives only the noise, whereas the primary microphone receives the speech,
the noise filtered by the acoustic path, and a small sensor noise $n_s$. The two inputs are expressed
as,
\begin{align}
x[n] &= v[n], \label{eq:x}\\
d[n] &= s[n] + \sum_{j=0}^{M-1} h_j\, v[n-j] + n_s[n]. \label{eq:d}
\end{align}
Let the last $L$ reference samples be collected in $\mathbf{x}[n]=[x[n],\dots,x[n-L+1]]^T$ and the
weights in $\mathbf{w}[n]=[w_0[n],\dots,w_{L-1}[n]]^T$. The filter estimates the noise component of
$d[n]$, and the remaining part forms the system output, which is given by,
\begin{align}
y[n] &= \mathbf{w}^T[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n] = \sum_{i=0}^{L-1} w_i[n]\,x[n-i], \label{eq:y}\\
e[n] &= d[n]-y[n]. \label{eq:e}
\end{align}
Since $s$ is uncorrelated with $v$, minimizing the mean-square error, which is formulated as,
\begin{align}
J(\mathbf{w}) &= E\{e^2[n]\} = E\{d^2[n]\} - 2\,\mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{w}^T\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}, \label{eq:J}\\
\mathbf{R} &= E\{\mathbf{x}[n]\,\mathbf{x}^T[n]\},\qquad \mathbf{p}=E\{d[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n]\}, \label{eq:Rp}
\end{align}
removes the noise and leaves the speech unaffected. Here, $\mathbf{R}$ denotes the autocorrelation
matrix of the reference vector and $\mathbf{p}$ denotes the cross-correlation vector between $d[n]$ and
$\mathbf{x}[n]$. The minimum of this cost is the Wiener solution $\mathbf{w}_o=\mathbf{R}^{-1}\mathbf{p}$,
which equals the acoustic path when $L\ge M$ and $n_s$ is white.

\subsection{LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift}
In LMS, the gradient $\nabla J=-2(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w})$ is replaced with its instantaneous
estimate $-2e[n]\mathbf{x}[n]$. All the three algorithms use the resulting update, which is expressed
as,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}[n+1]=\mathbf{w}[n]+\mu[n]\,e[n]\,\mathbf{x}[n].
\label{eq:upd}
\end{equation}
The three algorithms differ only in the step $\mu[n]$. With the window power $P[n]=\|\mathbf{x}[n]\|^2$,
the step sizes of LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift are given by,
\begin{align}
\mu_{\text{LMS}}[n] &= \mu, & 0<\mu&<\frac{2}{L\,\sigma_x^2}, \label{eq:mu_lms}\\
\mu_{\text{NLMS}}[n] &= \frac{\tilde\mu}{P[n]+\epsilon}, & 0<\tilde\mu&<2, \label{eq:mu_nlms}\\
\mu_{\text{Shift}}[n] &= 2^{-(k[n]+K)}, && \label{eq:mu_shift}\\
k[n] &= \lfloor\log_2(P[n]+\epsilon)\rfloor+1, && \label{eq:k}
\end{align}
where $\sigma_x^2$ denotes the reference variance and $\epsilon>0$ represents a small regularizer. The
LMS bound varies with the input level, and hence a fixed $\mu$ becomes either too slow for quiet inputs
or unstable for loud inputs. In contrast, the NLMS bound does not vary. In the hardware, the power is
updated with the exact recursion,
\begin{equation}
P[n] = P[n-1] + x^2[n] - x^2[n-L],
\label{eq:prec}
\end{equation}
which needs two squaring operations per sample instead of $L$. In NLMS-Shift, the exponent $k[n]$ is
simply the bit length of $P[n]+\epsilon$. This exponent is found by a leading-one detector, and the
multiplication by $\mu[n]$ becomes an arithmetic right shift of $e[n]\,x[n-i]$ by $k[n]+K$ places.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bound}
For every $n$, the NLMS-Shift step \eqref{eq:mu_shift} satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{2^{-K}}{2\,(P[n]+\epsilon)} \;\le\; \mu_{\text{Shift}}[n] \;<\; \frac{2^{-K}}{P[n]+\epsilon},
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
In other words, the NLMS-Shift step never falls more than a factor of two below the NLMS step with
$\tilde\mu=2^{-K}$, and it satisfies the NLMS stability bound $0<\tilde\mu<2$ for $K\ge0$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Proof.} By \eqref{eq:k}, $2^{k[n]-1}\le P[n]+\epsilon<2^{k[n]}$. Taking reciprocals and
multiplying by $2^{-K}$ gives \eqref{eq:bound}, since $\mu_{\text{Shift}}[n]=2^{-K}\,2^{-k[n]}$.\hfill$\square$

\smallskip\noindent The exact relation between the two steps is expressed as,
\begin{equation}
\mu[n]=\frac{r[n]\,2^{-K}}{P[n]+\epsilon},\qquad
r[n]=\frac{P[n]+\epsilon}{2^{\lfloor\log_2(P[n]+\epsilon)\rfloor+1}}\in[0.5,1),
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
Hence, NLMS-Shift is NLMS with the time-varying step $2^{-K}r[n]$ (Section~\ref{sec:equiv}). When the
input level is unknown over several octaves, the fractional part
$u=\log_2(P+\epsilon)-\lfloor\log_2(P+\epsilon)\rfloor$ is spread uniformly over $[0,1)$, and the mean
ratio is given by,
\begin{equation}
E\{r\}=\int_0^1 2^{u-1}\,du=\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\approx 0.72 .
\label{eq:er}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Fixed-point model}
The signals and the weights are represented as signed $Q1.F$ numbers with $W=12$ bits and $F=W-1$
fraction bits. Using the rounding shift and the saturation functions defined as,
\begin{align}
\operatorname{rs}(a,m) &= \left\lfloor \frac{a+2^{m-1}}{2^m}\right\rfloor, \label{eq:rs}\\
\operatorname{sat}_B(a) &= \min\!\left(\max\!\left(a,-2^{B-1}\right),\,2^{B-1}-1\right), \label{eq:sat}
\end{align}
the integer datapath operates on the integer samples $x_i=x[n-i]\cdot2^F$ and the weights $w_i$ as
follows,
\begin{align}
a[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_{B_a}\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{L-1} w_i\,x_i\Big), \label{eq:acc}\\
B_a &= 2W+\lceil\log_2 L\rceil, \label{eq:ba}\\
y[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_W\big(\operatorname{rs}(a[n],F)\big), \label{eq:yq}\\
e[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_W\big(d[n]-y[n]\big), \label{eq:ye}\\
w_i &\leftarrow \operatorname{sat}_W\big(w_i+\Delta w_i\big), \label{eq:wupd}
\end{align}
where the weight increments of LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift are given by,
\begin{align}
\Delta w_i^{\text{LMS}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big((M_\mu\,e[n])\,x_i,\;F+10\big), \label{eq:dw_lms}\\
\Delta w_i^{\text{NLMS}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big(g[n]\,x_i,\;F\big), \label{eq:dw_nlms}\\
g[n] &= \operatorname{sat}_{W+4}\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2N[n]+Q[n]}{2Q[n]}\right\rfloor\right), \label{eq:g}\\
\Delta w_i^{\text{Shift}} &= \operatorname{rs}\big(e[n]\,x_i,\;b(Q[n])-F+K\big), \label{eq:dw_shift}
\end{align}
Here, $\mu=M_\mu\,2^{-10}$, $N[n]=M_\mu\,e[n]\,2^{2F-10}$, $Q[n]=P[n]+2^{2F-E}$ for $\epsilon=2^{-E}$,
and $b(\cdot)$ denotes the bit length of a positive integer. Every point at which a value is narrowed
saturates and sets a sticky flag, so that an overflow is never left unnoticed. The integer model
(\texttt{py/fixedpoint.py}, \texttt{py/rtl\_model.py}) uses exactly these widths and generates the golden
vectors.

\section{Hardware architecture}\label{sec:hw}
\subsection{Core}\label{sec:core}
The datapath of the core (\texttt{rtl/anc\_core.v}, vendor-neutral Verilog) is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:core}. The core accepts one sample pair per \texttt{in\_valid} strobe and performs all its
arithmetic on a single multiplier with a three-stage pipeline (operand registers, product register and
consume stage). A finite-state machine steps through each sample. It spends two cycles on squaring the
new and the dropped reference samples for \eqref{eq:prec} (NLMS and NLMS-Shift only), $L$ cycles on the
filter sum \eqref{eq:acc} and one cycle on $y$ and $e$ \eqref{eq:ye}. Thereafter, the step computation is
carried out, followed by $L$ cycles of weight updates \eqref{eq:wupd}. NLMS adds a sequential restoring
divider for the gain $g[n]$, whereas NLMS-Shift uses a leading-one detector and a shift-amount register in
its place. A \texttt{freeze} input holds the weights while the filtering continues.

\begin{\WF}[!htbp]\centering
\adjustbox{max width=\linewidth}{%
\input{fig_core_tikz}}
\caption{Datapath of the ANC core. A single multiplier is shared by the power update, the filter sum, the
step computation and the weight updates, and only the step unit differs between the three algorithms.
Dashed lines denote control signals.}\label{fig:core}
\end{\WF}

This schedule fixes both the input-to-output latency and the number of busy cycles per sample, which
are expressed as,
\begin{align}
N_{\text{lat}} &= \begin{cases} L+3 & \text{LMS},\\ L+5 & \text{NLMS, NLMS-Shift},\end{cases} \label{eq:lat}\\
N_{\text{busy}} &= \begin{cases} 2L+9 & \text{LMS},\\ 2L+13+N_{\text{div}} & \text{NLMS},\\ 2L+8 & \text{NLMS-Shift},\end{cases} \label{eq:busy}
\end{align}
The restoring divider performs one iteration per dividend bit,
$N_{\text{div}}=W+B_\mu+2F+4+\lceil\log_2 L\rceil$, where $B_\mu$ denotes the signed width of $M_\mu$, and
hence the NLMS cycle count increases with the step-size constant. For $L=32$ and the tuned settings, the
above formulas give latencies of 35, 37 and 37 cycles and busy times of 73, 130 ($N_{\text{div}}=53$) and
72 cycles, which are the same values measured on the board (Section~\ref{sec:board}).

\subsection{Board wrapper}
The DE2-115 wrapper around the core contains an I$^2$C engine that configures the WM8731 at reset (11
registers, each write being acknowledge-checked), an I$^2$S receiver and transmitter, two-flop
synchronizers on the reset and the switches, a frame-latched bypass multiplexer, an overrun flag, and four
registered test points on the GPIO header. The I$^2$S blocks run on the 50~MHz system clock and sample the
bit and word clocks of the codec as data. The codec acts as the clock master and is driven from a
12.5~MHz clock with its sample-rate register set to 1536~fs. Therefore, the sample rate and the number of
clock cycles per sample are given by,
\begin{align}
f_s &= \frac{f_{\text{MCLK}}}{1536}=\frac{12.5~\text{MHz}}{1536}=8.138~\text{kHz}, \label{eq:fs}\\
N_s &= \frac{f_{\text{clk}}}{f_s}=\frac{50~\text{MHz}}{8.138~\text{kHz}}=6144, \label{eq:ns}
\end{align}
where $f_s$ is the rate used in the simulations and $N_s$ is the number of clock cycles per sample. The
fraction of each sample period during which the core is busy is expressed as,
\begin{equation}
U=\frac{N_{\text{busy}}}{N_s}
\label{eq:util}
\end{equation}
From \eqref{eq:busy}, this fraction is 1.19\%, 2.12\% and 1.17\% for LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift,
respectively. As mentioned earlier, no processor or vendor IP is involved.

\section{Experimental set-up}\label{sec:setup}
The synthetic scenarios follow the reference model $x=v$, $d=s+h*v+n_s$ at 8~kHz with 16,000 samples.
Four scenarios are considered, namely stationary colored noise, amplitude steps ($\times3$ followed by
$\times0.25$), an abrupt path switch at the midpoint, and a recorded chord as the noise source. The step
sizes and the regularizers were selected on seeds 101--103 by maximizing the mean final noise reduction
over the stationary, amplitude-step and path-switch scenarios, with no overflow allowed. This selection
gave $\mu=0.25$ for LMS; $\mu=0.25$ and $\epsilon=1$ for NLMS; and $K=3$ and $\epsilon=0.25$ for
NLMS-Shift. All the reported results use seeds 1--20. The final noise reduction is computed over the last
quarter $\mathcal{N}$ of the samples as,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NR}=10\log_{10}\frac{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\big(d[n]-s[n]\big)^2}{\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\big(e[n]-s[n]\big)^2}\ \text{dB},
\label{eq:nr}
\end{equation}
and each table entry gives the mean over the $S=20$ seeds along with its 95\% confidence interval,
which is given by,
\begin{equation}
\overline{\mathrm{NR}}\pm t_{0.975,\,S-1}\,\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{NR}}}{\sqrt{S}},\qquad t_{0.975,19}=2.093 .
\label{eq:ci}
\end{equation}
For comparing two algorithms A and B, the paired per-seed differences
$\Delta_k=\mathrm{NR}_{\mathrm{A},k}-\mathrm{NR}_{\mathrm{B},k}$ are used. The two algorithms are
considered equivalent when two one-sided tests (TOST) both reject, at $p<0.05$, the hypothesis that the
mean difference lies outside a margin $\pm\delta$ with $\delta=0.5$~dB, where the two null hypotheses are
expressed as,
\begin{equation}
H_{01}:\ \bar\Delta\le-\delta,\qquad H_{02}:\ \bar\Delta\ge\delta .
\label{eq:tost}
\end{equation}
\emph{Use of generative AI.} During this work, the authors used Claude (Anthropic) as an assistant to
write and debug the analysis scripts and testbench code, to search for and summarize the literature, and
to draft and edit the text of this manuscript. \TBD{Authors: add any other AI tools you used (for example
for local runs or literature searches) and adjust the list above if needed.} The authors checked every
reported number against the committed result files and board captures, verified each reference against
its source, and take full responsibility for the content of this article.

For the real recordings, four noise files (air conditioner, vacuum cleaner, copy machine and babble)
and ten utterances were taken from the public MS-SNSD
corpus\footnote{\url{https://github.com/microsoft/MS-SNSD}} and resampled to 8~kHz. In each seed, a noise
segment, an utterance and a random 16-tap acoustic path are drawn at 0~dB input SNR, both with and without
speech leaking into the reference at $-20$~dB. The noise reduction, the short-time objective
intelligibility (STOI) measure and the narrow-band perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ, ITU-T
Recommendation P.862) are computed after a 2~s convergence window.

For all four real noises (Table~\ref{tab:real}), the filter roughly doubles the PESQ and brings the
STOI close to 1. The ranking of the algorithms remains the same in every case: NLMS leads LMS by
0.4--0.6~dB, and NLMS-Shift trails NLMS by 0.15--0.35~dB. The noise reduction is lower than that in
Table~\ref{tab:nr}, but this is not because the real noise is harder to predict. When the speech was
removed from the primary input, the same filters on the same noise segments and paths achieved
18.5--19.2~dB on the air conditioner and 22.4--31.9~dB on the other three noises, which is equal to or
higher than the reduction on synthetic stationary noise
(\texttt{py/\allowbreak exp\_real\_audio\_nospeech.py}, \texttt{results/\allowbreak metrics/\allowbreak real\_audio\_nospeech.csv}).
Although the speech is uncorrelated with the reference, it still enters the error $e[n]$ that drives the
weight update, and it costs 6.4--17.9~dB. Without speech, LMS is ahead of both normalized variants on all
four noises, whereas with speech it loses the most (7.6--17.9~dB against 6.4--14.8~dB for NLMS), and hence
the order is reversed. The usual remedy is to slow down or stop the adaptation while speech is present.
The \texttt{freeze} input of the core (Section~\ref{sec:core}) could be driven by a voice-activity
detector for this purpose, but this has not been evaluated in this work. When $-20$~dB of the speech
leaks into the reference, the filter also cancels a part of the speech. In this case, the noise reduction
drops by 2.3--4.6~dB and the PESQ by 0.25--0.6, and saturation events appear (18,500 over 19.2 million
processed samples, mostly for the air conditioner). Therefore, the position of the reference microphone
matters more than the choice of the algorithm (\texttt{paper/tab\_real\_audio\_xt.tex}).

\subsection{Word length}
At the tuned settings, the worst-scenario loss against a floating-point run of the same algorithm is
4.1--5.9~dB at $W=10$, 0.9--1.2~dB at $W=11$ and 0.3--0.4~dB at $W=12$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:wl}). Eleven bits
is the shortest word length that stays within the 3~dB criterion, and 12 bits were retained for margin.
The tonal chord recording shows an unusual behavior, in which the fixed-point filter outperforms floating
point by up to 3.4~dB at $W=11$ and loses 1.3~dB at $W=13$. On such a narrow-band signal, the
quantization changes the effective adaptation, and hence the chord result varies with the word length and
should not be used to select it.

\subsection{Equivalent step size of NLMS-Shift}\label{sec:equiv}
From \eqref{eq:ratio}, NLMS-Shift and NLMS differ only in the factor $r[n]$. When averaged over input
levels spread uniformly on a logarithmic scale, $E[r]=\int_0^1 2^{u-1}\,du=1/(2\ln 2)\approx0.72$. At any
single level, however, $r$ depends on the position of $P+\epsilon$ inside its octave: it increases with
the level and halves when $P+\epsilon$ crosses a power of two. With $L=32$ and $\epsilon=2^{-2}$, the
crossing $P+\epsilon=1/2$ occurs at a reference RMS of $-21.1$~dBFS, which is 3~dB above the level used in
the simulations. To examine this effect, the input level was swept over 12~dB in 0.75~dB steps in the
integer model (\texttt{py/exp\_equiv\_step.py}), on the stationary scenario (seeds 1--5) and on a two-tone
reference (Fig.~\ref{fig:equiv}). On broadband noise, the window power varies enough to smooth out the
sawtooth, and the mean ratio $\bar r$ changes gradually between 0.56 and 0.78. On the tonal reference,
$P$ is almost constant, and $\bar r$ drops from 0.87 to 0.62 within 0.75~dB of level. In both cases, NLMS
with the constant step $\bar r\,2^{-K}$ reproduces NLMS-Shift. The final noise reduction agrees within
0.27~dB at all 17 broadband levels, and within 0.55~dB at every tonal level except the one that straddles
the crossing (1.7~dB), while the convergence time agrees within 8\%. In contrast, NLMS with the nominal
step $2^{-K}$ deviates by up to 3.5~dB and 48\%.

Hence, the shift changes nothing in the adaptation except the step size. It converts the fixed NLMS
step into a step between $0.5$ and $1$ times the nominal step, which is set by the input level. On
broadband input, this is a smooth change of gain, and selecting $K$ (and $\epsilon$) for the expected
level absorbs it. On tonal input, the step changes by up to a factor of about 0.7 when the level crosses a
power-of-two boundary of $P+\epsilon$, which never happens in a fixed-step NLMS.

LMS is about 31\% smaller than either of the normalized variants. NLMS-Shift and NLMS require almost the
same logic (2,610 and 2,626 logic elements), since the area saved on the divider is consumed by the
leading-one detector, the shifter and the wider update path. However, NLMS-Shift has 15\% fewer
registers, needs 45\% fewer busy cycles per sample and toggles 16\% fewer bits per sample. All three
designs meet the 50~MHz constraint with a setup slack of at least +2.953~ns and a hold slack of
+0.401~ns, without any critical warnings. At 8.138~kHz, even the busiest variant uses only 2\% of the
6,144-cycle sample period, and hence a single core could serve many channels or much longer filters.

The power values are Quartus vectorless estimates with the default toggle rate of 12.5\%, which Quartus
itself rates as low confidence. The core static power (about 98.7~mW) dominates the total power of 169
to 173~mW. The dynamic estimates rank the designs as LMS $<$ NLMS-Shift $<$ NLMS, which is the same order
as the toggle counts. However, a fixed toggle rate mostly tracks the area and ignores the fact that the
datapath remains idle for 98\% of each sample period, and hence these values are upper bounds and not the
energy per sample. A gate-level, activity-annotated estimate is left for future work, because the
post-fit netlist flattens the hierarchy observed by the top-level testbench, and a netlist-only testbench
is therefore required. A fair comparison with Huang and Yao~\cite{huang2026}, who used the same board,
would report the energy per sample as the dynamic power divided by the sample rate from an
activity-annotated Power Analyzer run. Since this result is not yet available, no energy comparison with
their design is claimed. Moreover, their design is fully parallel and runs at megasamples per second,
whereas ULLAP shares one multiplier and runs at 8.138~kHz, so the area and energy have to be compared per
tap and per sample rather than as raw totals.



\subsection{On-board results}\label{sec:board}
The board evidence was recorded with Signal Tap on the FPGA itself, and hence no external recorder or
oscilloscope was required. One logic-analyzer instance stores, once per sample, the two codec samples
entering the core ($x$ and $d$, register outputs \texttt{x\_reg} and \texttt{d\_reg}) and the core output
$e$. This instance uses a transitional storage qualifier on \texttt{out\_valid}, triggers when the reset
button is released, and holds 32,768 samples (4.0~s). A second instance samples the four GPIO test points
at 50~MHz. The stimuli were the noise-only versions of the four scenarios, which were played from a laptop
into LINE IN after a 1~kHz calibration tone. The reset was pressed during the tone, so that every capture
starts from zero weights on real codec data at about $-25$~dBFS, and no sample in any capture reaches
full scale. The analysis script and the raw exports are available in the repository
(\texttt{py/analyze\_board\_capture.py}, \texttt{board\_evidence/}).

\emph{Bit-exactness.} For each of the 12 captures (three algorithms and four scenarios), the integer
model of Section~\ref{sec:model} was started from zero state at the first sample after the reset and was
fed with the captured $x$ and $d$. The model reproduced every captured $e[n]$, i.e., 351,723 samples in
total and 28,672 to 32,768 (3.5 to 4.0~s) per capture, without a single mismatch. Therefore, the
fixed-point arithmetic on the chip is exactly the arithmetic evaluated in simulation, even with an analog
input that carries the noise and offsets of the codec.

\emph{Timing.} The GPIO capture shows an \texttt{in\_valid} period of 6,144 clock cycles
(8,138.02~Hz), an \texttt{in\_valid} to \texttt{out\_valid} spacing of 38, 36 and 38 cycles for
NLMS-Shift, LMS and NLMS (the core latency plus one accept cycle, i.e., 0.76, 0.72 and 0.76~$\mu$s), and
busy times of 72, 73 and 130 cycles. All these values are equal to those of the RTL simulation with the
same parameters. The builds with Signal Tap still meet the 50~MHz constraint (setup slack of $+2.65$,
$+5.68$ and $+3.78$~ns), while Table~\ref{tab:hw} refers to the builds without the logic analyzer.

Fig.~\ref{fig:board} shows the three algorithms on the same amplitude-step stimulus, aligned to the
start of the scenario. (Since KEY0 was pressed by hand during the calibration tone, the reset occurs at a
slightly different point of the stimulus in each capture.) All three algorithms settle to about 15~dB in
the first 1.5~s. At 2.1~s, the level of $d$ rises by 7.4~dB, and the algorithms follow the step with only
a short burst in $e$. The residual power after the step ($-47$ to $-52$~dBFS) is lower than that before
the step ($-44$~dBFS), and hence the noise reduction rises to 25 to 30~dB. Over the last quarter of the
window, the reduction is about 40~dB below 1.5~kHz and 11 to 15~dB near the band edge (3.5 to
3.9~kHz).

\emph{Noise reduction on the simulation signal.} In a second session, the 2~s, seed-1 noise-only signal
of each scenario, i.e., exactly the 16,000-sample signal used in the simulations, was played once after
the calibration tone, so that the complete scenario fits inside the capture. Its start was located by
correlating the captured $x$ with the stimulus (correlation of 0.95 to 0.99), and
$\mathrm{NR}_\mathrm{final}$ was computed over the same last 25~\% as in the simulation. All 12 captures
(28,672 samples after the reset each) are again bit-exact. Table~\ref{tab:board} compares the board with
the integer model on the same signal. The board results are 2.9 to 10.6~dB below the simulation, by
similar amounts for all three algorithms. The cause of this difference is the input level and not the
hardware. The codec delivered $x$ and $d$ at about $-27$~dBFS, which is 3~dB below the simulation level,
and at the tuned step sizes a lower input power slows down the adaptation within the 2~s. When the model
is run at the level actually received by the board, it matches the board within 0.2~dB on stationary and
path-switch noise (Fig.~\ref{fig:boardlevel}). The amplitude-step and recorded-noise scenarios end with a
quiet final quarter ($d$ at about $-41$ and $-57$~dBFS), and in these cases the board stays 1.7 to
8.1~dB below the level-matched model. At these levels, two analog effects are no longer small compared
with $d$: the noise of the playback and codec path ($-70$~dBFS, measured in the silence after the
stimulus) and an ADC offset that decays during the first second after the reset. For amplitude steps,
even the best 32-tap least-squares filter fitted to the board samples of that window reaches only 24~dB,
against 28~dB on the ideal signal. On the board, the three algorithms remain within 0.7~dB of one another
in every scenario.

\emph{Freeze, DAC word and endurance.} With NLMS-Shift running on stationary noise, SW[1] was flipped
by hand 1.44~s into the scenario. The captured output is equal to the model with the adaptation stopped
from that sample onwards (28,672 samples, no mismatch). From the switch to the end of the scenario, the
noise reduction holds at 15.2~dB, against 15.4~dB just before the switch and 15.9~dB if the adaptation
had continued. A third Signal Tap instance recorded the word sent to the DAC. In ANC mode, this word
equals $\{e, 0000\}$, and in bypass mode ($\mathrm{SW}[0]=1$) its upper 12 bits equal $d$, in all 1,792
samples of each capture. The LINE OUT signal could not be recorded with the available equipment, and
hence this check covers the chain only up to the DAC interface. After 10~minutes of continuous operation
on looped noise, the GPIO timing remained unchanged (6,144 cycles per sample, 38 cycles from
\texttt{in\_valid} to \texttt{out\_valid} and 72 busy cycles), and no saturation, NACK or overrun flag was
set. The build with all three logic-analyzer instances still met timing (slack of $+2.93$~ns).

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:disc}
The comparison in this work supports a narrow conclusion. The divider-free normalization retains the
level independence of NLMS at roughly the cycle count of LMS. Therefore, it is the better choice when the
noise level changes and when throughput or energy per sample is important, whereas LMS remains the
smallest design when the level is steady. The comparison does not show that NLMS-Shift cancels better
than NLMS, and Section~\ref{sec:equiv} explains the reason. The shift changes only the step size, by a
level-dependent factor between 0.5 and 1, and hence selecting $K$ and $\epsilon$ for the expected input
level matters more than the shift itself. In addition, tonal noise near a power-of-two boundary of
$P+\epsilon$ experiences a step jump that is absent in NLMS. On real recordings, the speech at the primary
input limits all three algorithms much more than the choice of the algorithm does (6.4--17.9~dB, against
at most 0.6~dB between the algorithms).

The present study has certain limitations. The acoustic paths are simulated, including those used for
the real recordings. Acoustic feedback (closed-loop howling) is not covered, and reference-based ANC is
not a suitable structure for this problem in any case. The power is only a switching-activity proxy until
a gate-level analysis is carried out. On the board, the output is shown to be equal to the model up to the
DAC word, but the analog LINE OUT signal was not recorded. Finally, the absolute noise reduction depends on
the input level. With the stimulus 3~dB below the simulation level, the board achieved 14 to 17~dB instead
of 19 to 28~dB on the synthetic scenarios, and on stationary and path-switch noise the model reproduces
this result at that level. Therefore, in a practical deployment, the codec input gain should be set such
that the reference uses most of the 12-bit range, or the step size should be retuned for the expected
level.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
In this research, ULLAP is presented, which runs LMS, NLMS and NLMS-Shift on a single serial 12-bit,
32-tap core on a low-cost Cyclone IV board and compares the three algorithms with equal tuning effort.
NLMS-Shift matches NLMS within the 95\% confidence interval on stationary and amplitude-step noise, while
needing 72 instead of 130 busy cycles per sample. All three algorithms remove 11.7 to 14.9~dB of real
recorded noise, and 18.5 to 31.9~dB when the speech is removed from the primary input, which shows that
the speech, and not the noise, sets the limit. Moreover, NLMS-Shift is shown to be equivalent to NLMS with
a level-dependent step between 0.5 and 1 times the nominal step. On the board, 25 Signal Tap captures of
real codec data (724,459 samples) are bit-exact with the integer model, and the same holds for the
freeze behavior and the DAC word. On stationary and path-switch noise, the measured noise reduction is
equal to that of the model, at the input level received by the board, within 0.2~dB.
